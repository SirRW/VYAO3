### Text

*I believe that what we become depends on what our fathers teach us at odd moments, when* *they aren't trying to teach us. We are formed by little scraps of wisdom*.

Umberto Eco

*Viktor, eleven*

The air down here clings to the back of the throat sluggishly, heavy with dust and chemicals. If the ventilation system is problematic, if gas builds up, if someone is careless—accidents happen all the time in the Undercity, it is nothing new.

It’s still shocking when it happens to him.

When the mines collapse, the earth trembles miles away; the atmosphere grows so toxic, it doesn’t clear out for a week. Viktor spends most of the aftermath coughing and sobbing. Did the ground shake all the way to Piltover? It hasn’t stopped moving under his feet yet—bones rattling in his body, horror in his unsteady heart, death in his lungs.

Once Viktor is brave enough to inquire about *My* *parents…?* a member from the rescue team shakes her head at him. Coal smudges on her cheeks, eyes red-rimmed. He wants to spell their names for her anyway—*There must be a mistake, please*—just so he can say them out loud, just to make sure, and she checks the list again out of pity.

‘I’m so sorry, kiddo.’ And it is sincere, she sounds like it. Dark purple hair covered in dust, gaze filled with sorrow. He’s seen her before, hanging around the Last Drop with the kind of crowd his parents would discuss over dinner. ‘I worked with your folks—they were good and honest people.’

Viktor’s mother would steal medicine from the pharmacy on the bank of the river. Viktor’s father would hold the owner at knife point, his arm trembling. He doesn’t know about *good* and *honest*—principals lose ground when poverty comes into the equation. He desperately wants the luxury to be what his parents always wished for him: great.

He nods and turns to look at the ruined adit, raising his shirt to cover his nose. The heat it emits is unbearable, the smoke thick, and there is something alluring about its blackness, as if calling him to step closer. Death beckoning, a dark door opening wider. He wants to cross over the threshold; nothing will ever be okay again. He wants to run as fast as possible, but he knows his feet won’t carry him too far away. And so he stares, enraptured, and wonders.

They will divert the river and flood the mines. A water grave, until the colliery can be drained, and then work will resume. The Underground is counting losses, not survivors; the world can never stop for too long if there are needs to be met.

He builds a small steamboat and test-runs it on the stream that must have washed his parents’ ashes away. The invention guides him from the bright light of the oasis towards the dim darkness of a cave. It is damp and cool, and the rocks he stabilises himself with as he tries to catch up with the boat are slimy with grime and ivy. Viktor can hear his laboured breath, the tumbling of pebbles into the water as he knocks them over in his haste, the distant rash of an underground river.

The doctor is a solitary companion who takes an orphan child under his wing and teaches him everything he thinks he knows. Viktor must learn everything, so he does: mathematics— algebra, geometry. Other things too: what do waveriders eat, what is suitable for Rio’s disease, what climate is best for her to grow better. Knowledge is limitless, knowledge is powerful. Viktor absorbs information eagerly, with despair that stems from loss. Who will take care of Rio if the doctor dies? If anything happens to him, Viktor will be responsible for keeping Rio alive and happy. He will take care of Rio when everyone else is gone.

He’s learnt about homeostasis; living organisms maintain a stable internal environment despite changes in external conditions. Example: waveriders will store water in their bloodstream to withstand extreme dehydration. Viktor thinks of a self-regulating system balancing out wrongs: if he is able to right this wrong against Rio, it will make up for what happened to him. It is an alternative hypothesis, proposes that there is a relationship between variables. Stability, symmetry are innate to all creatures—why shouldn’t perfection be too?

Different kinds of lessons, too, ingrained in the mind: loneliness is often the byproduct of a gifted mind. Others will ostracise you, reject your talents, dismiss your ambition*. You cannot* *let that stop you, Viktor, you mustn’t.* There is no limit to what science can achieve, and therefore no limit to what a scientist can do. When nobody believes in your dream, you believe in yourself. When you’re going to change the world, don’t ask for permission. He learns those teachings best, knows them to be axioms, that which commands itself as evident. And though soon enough Viktor finds out the doctor’s cruel methods and runs away, his influence follows him like a starving dog, stored in a child’s neurons to withstand extreme conditions. Homeostasis, again: a living organism will maintain a stable environment to survive, survive, survive against anything.

*Viktor, twelve*

He looks for work—

Showing the old lady at the trinket shop the pocket watch he found and fixed; it shines under the dim light, its golden chain wrapped around thin, long fingers. Her eyes narrowing on his ragged clothes, the dirt under his nails. Wrinkling her nose in suspicion, *Did you steal this; I* *don’t need trouble from the Enforces, run along*

He looks for work, but there are obstacles—

In the pharmacy near the river bank, demonstrating his knowledge of math and what little chemistry he learnt from the doctor, that stupid steamboat he should have smashed to pieces, because he is not a child, he doesn’t play. The owner’s apologetic gaze taking in Viktor, his young age but older face, *We only hire Academy graduates, have you even finished school*

He looks for work, but nobody hires cripples—

In the old factory in the fissures, the one with the stability issues and the broken windows and the chemical pollution, the manager with the dead eyes that don’t meet Viktor’s, only focus on the limp and the cane and all the ways he is sick, all the ways he is *Not suited for this kind* *of work, son—with that foot, you will only keep production behind*

He looks for work, but but *but* is found lacking—

Neon red lights and heavy, sweet perfume that chokes him, and too much skin, unreadable looks and lingering touches, until he’s in the yordle’s office, and then it’s red furniture and silk, a sad smile and a *What are you looking for, dear, you’re way too young, maybe in a few* *years,*

He wants to say he wouldn’t be asking if he weren’t desperate and hungry and hopeless. He wants to say he has nowhere else to go; he can’t turn to the mines, because the earth swallowed his parents, and if he went, he might let it swallow him too.

Faceless strangers—sneering, pitying, indifferent, kind—saying he’s not enough. Their voices blur into each other until he’s no longer sure which are their words and his thoughts. His posture slumps, his knee hurts, his body shuts down one day and is relatively okay the other, and his skin grows thin and pale with sickness and malnutrition. The reflection of the boy staring back at Viktor in the oasis where he once played is hollow and hungry and losing a battle within a system made to work against him.

He only has to believe in himself, he only has to remember there are no limits, but those teachings prove to be futile in the face of extreme poverty, in these circumstances. Homeostasis fails him. The body cannot sustain or regulate its needs. The body is defective, imperfect. The body cannot save him; the city will kill him. No one cares, only Viktor must, but he does not have the strength or the will anymore.

The thought flashes through his head, and it’s the most coherent one he’s had in days: the mines. (Didn’t he have a reason not to go there? Does it matter?) He has to get to the mines, because mum and dad are late, they should have returned an hour ago, when their shift ends, but they’re not here. They are not here, so Viktor will go to them; drag them back to the neon lights of the Undercity, instead of the weak lights beneath the ground where you can barely see in front of you. Or, or—if he can’t, if he’s too weak, maybe he can join them down below, maybe they will let him stay. They will understand, won’t they? He is tired.

He finds himself wandering around the adit, stumbling on nothing, scanning the crowd emerging from the colliery, looking for his family. With each person passing, he doesn’t see them, the crowd clears; then the adit is black, and nobody is coming out anymore. The dark door in his mind opens wider, and Viktor steps close, hand numb from clutching his cane. He could cross the threshold and just keep walking until his lungs or his legs or his heart gives out. He could join his parents and let himself lean on his mother’s side, and let his father ruffle his hair, and he wouldn’t even complain.

*That doesn’t sound so bad now, does it?* says his mother, her cold, bloody hand cradling his cheek. He grabs her wrist, and his hand touches nothing. Her head tilts, like a confused child’s, before the hallucination fades, and his vision turns black.

‘Mamka?’ is the first word out of his mouth when he has a lucid moment. Scared and unsure and low, because he knows it’s not her, because she’s gone, both are.

‘I’m not she, child.’ The stranger, sitting on a chair beside the bed, legs crossed, tilts his head, jet black hair falling in his face, and it is like a record screeching inside his mind, because Viktor is lying on a bed, propped up on his elbows, and at the same time his mother is capping his face and cocking her head. Ringing dissonance in his ears, he falls back on the mattress, exhausted.

A younger face, all sharp edges like a razor. Blue-green eyes meet his own, and Viktor inhales shakily. This is the man from the Last Drop, hanging around the woman with the purple hair and the Hound. The kind of crowd his parents discussed over dinner, with the kind of plans they didn’t want to get involved in.

‘You don’t work in the mines.’

Viktor shakes his head.

‘Where did you come from then?’ The man is tapping a finger on his chin. A hint of mirth in his eyes.

‘I was looking for work.’

‘At the end of the shift? Surely not. What’s your name?’

‘Viktor.’

‘Why were you looking for work, Viktor?’

‘My parents are dead, sir. Are you hiring, why do you ask?’ Viktor clutches the blanket and sneaks a glance around the room for his cane; it’s propped up on the opposite wall.

There is a glass of water on the nightstand, a desk crammed against the wall, a precariously stable spinning chair. The window looking out the street is broken, and the green-tinted light enters in strange jagged shapes on the floorboards. A faint conversation from below; they are on an upper floor.

‘Children should never have to work.’ The man sighs, something unreadable in his look— anger? It’s been a while since anyone was angry on Viktor’s behalf. The inscrutable emotion ages the man by a decade. Half-covered in green shade, he offers his hand like he would to an adult. ‘I am Silco.’

Viktor starts hanging around Silco, and by extension, around the Last Drop, and no one bats an eye. The woman with the purple hair—Felicia, he learns—grins when she sees him and says Silco is growing soft. Vander lays a hand on his shoulder, *Lad, you’re always welcome* *here*, and Viktor sucks in a shaky breath, because this is too good. Perfection requires effort and hypotheses and experiments. But he hasn’t tested his hypothesis, something is bound to go wrong, so he keeps waiting for the other shoe to drop. Those like him are always alone, not meant to be a part of something larger. You only get one family.

Silco had asked *Who takes care of you,* and Viktor had thought of the doctor and shook his head, and Silco said *Stay here until you get* *better*, which eventually turned to *Stay here* *indefinitely*. And he doesn’t know what to do with that, so he babysits Felicia’s daughter when she is working, and he fixes up vents and clocks and the cash register, and he looks over the book-keeping and the oil lamp so that it doesn’t burn out. He wants to stay. He wants to be useful, to be enough.

‘You want to help, little one?’ Silco sits him down one day and asks.

‘Yes,’ said quickly, decisively.

Viktor spins on the stool in front of the bar while Silco goes around the bar. He stops spinning, dangles his bad foot in the air, hugs the other close to his chest. Silco drums his fingers on the counter, sitting with his back straight, and Viktor tries to mimic him. Piercing eyes hold his gaze; Viktor swallows, looks down.

‘Children of Zaun will always find help here,’ Silco says. ‘We fight for each other, we stand up for our people.’ A light touch on his chin, raising it up, and Viktor sees that he is smiling. ‘You don’t have to earn your place, boy.’

He thinks two things: one, loneliness is often the byproduct of a gifted mind, so how can he just belong here? Two, he couldn’t help anyone before, not his parents, not Rio, not himself. And now that he is able to, now that he can—Viktor doesn’t want to sit around, do nothing and take; he wants to give back. He needs to give back.

Silco seems to follow his trail of thought. ‘I said you don’t have to; I didn’t say you can’t,’ he says—careful, thoughtful.

‘But I want to help you.’

‘You already are, child. Listen to me; it is enough, it is perfect.’

*Perfect*. Something unnameable tightens around his throat, loosens up, and he can breathe.

How to explain he wants to help the way he wanted to help Rio, the way he wanted someone to have helped him? Balancing wrongs out; homeostasis all over again. The Undercity is a giant living organism, trying to preserve itself under any circumstance; if he doesn’t do his part, the body might fail. He knows the system is already malfunctioning. Viktor is not sure he can articulate this, though.

‘No.’

A raised eyebrow. ‘No?’

‘I want to do what you do. For the Underground. For our people.’ It’s words he has heard, words he’s repeating.

Silco appears so frozen that Viktor starts to wonder if he said something wrong, squirming under the scrutiny. The lamps above the counter illuminate them both in a warm yellow light, and it makes Silco look softer, somehow: his edges not so sharp, his eyes not so piercing, but bright and familiar.

‘I think your parents would be very proud of you,’ he eventually says. He takes an empty cup from a shelf and pours juice in it, slides it to Viktor.

Privately, Viktor thinks they would be terrified. But he wraps his hands around the cup and listens, because Silco speaks of the nation of Zaun, of independence, of breaking free from Piltover and starting from zero to build a better future. No Enforcers beating their way through a crowd to break up a riot, vandalising small businesses whose owners displeased them, or simply because they could. No working beneath the earth where the air gets thin and the light trembles and the smell is so repulsive it fills the nose and makes the eyes water. No Councilor benefitting from the labour of underpaid workers who don’t control the distribution of their production. No starving children roaming the streets looking for something to eat when they should be at school learning—because education is a right. Because playing is a right. Being a child is a right.

‘We all have our parts to play in the rebellion,’ Silco says. ‘There is no cost too high for this cause, child.’ His gaze is directed behind Viktor, thin lips turning upwards.

A heavy hand lands on Viktor’s back, and he turns to find Vander smiling down at him.

‘Radicalising the boy, are you, Sil?’ He sounds amused as he joins Silco behind the counter; their shoulders brush, and Silco rolls his eyes fondly.

‘He is a clever one. I only want him to realise his own potential.’

Viktor smiles absent-mindedly. The doctor’s words linger in his head: *There is no limit to* *what science can achieve, and therefore no limit to what a scientist can do.* But now they are reinforced, somehow, bouncing on the walls of his mind, echoing along other, parallel teachings: *There is no cost too high for this cause, child*. And Viktor understands now that there is no line that should not be pushed to achieve what one seeks. There is no limit to what science can pursue; there is no threshold a rebellion cannot cross. Lessons taught start to blur into one solid understanding, a fundamental perception of the world. He must not wonder how far he is willing to go, he only needs to believe in what he is doing.

*Viktor, fourteen*

Their faces are out of focus, a yellowed picture on fragile paper; it might turn to dust, if he dares touch it. A cognitive nightmare of dissonant feedback: mouths out of sync with the distorted voices. His father says something, but all he hears is *There is no cost too high for* *what a believer can do*, which is wrong in so many ways—his father never said that, the doctor never said that, Silco never said that; lessons blend together, paint mixing with poison mixing with oil.

His mother tilts her head, a curtain of hair falling on her shoulder. Her hand reaches out, its lines not tangent, but crossing each other like a child’s sketch. *That doesn’t sound so bad,* *now, does it*, she echoes, otherworldly, and shaking his head, he repeats to himself that it was a hallucination caused by starvation, nothing more.

Viktor takes a fearful step back, his knee jerks, his leg crumples under him, and—

He wakes up covered in sweat; his bad leg is throbbing, and the pain reaches up to his spine. He tenses up, muscles spasming, and barely has the time to lean over the edge of the mattress and vomit on the floor. He heaves and gags, trembling hands digging into his knee, respiration running down his forehead.

Thudding footsteps up the stairs; the door opens gently. Viktor slumps on the bed as Silco and Felicia enter the room, watches them take in the scene, feels shame settle in his lungs. It is the middle of the night; they should be downstairs, scheming and spending time with their friends.

‘Bad day, huh?’ Felicia brushes a strand of hair off his face and checks his temperature. Silco stays back, palm on the doorknob, unmoving.

‘Eh, I’ve had worse.’ The pain is excruciating; he might pass out. The dream is burned behind his eyelids, though the details are fading. A hammer is steadily knocking on his temples. He has not had worse.

‘I can get you painkillers and something to eat when you’re feeling less like death warmed up?’

The idea of food makes him nauseous. ‘A glass of water, perhaps. And—yes, painkillers. Please.’

‘You got it, kiddo.’ She moves to rise, but Silco beats her to it and shuts the door behind him.

Felicia sighs and leans on the desk, peering outside the window. She takes note of the asphodel on the sill and walks a finger around the vase. Neon flashes and a chill peek into the room, before she closes the shutter, the noise from the street muffled.

‘Beautiful flower,’ she comments and props herself up on the sill. She looks worn thin, and he hates that she has to take care of him, hates that he can’t perfect his flaws like he fixed the bathroom sink.

‘Thank you. My mother had one home.’ His eyes move to the door; Felicia follows his gaze.

‘You haven’t done anything wrong. Silco’s upset you’re hurting, and he doesn’t know what to do with it.’

Viktor shrugs and hisses when sharp pain stabs his shoulderblades. ‘How is Violet?’ he asks. ‘I haven’t kept her company for a while now.’

‘Oh, she’s a formidable four-year-old either leading her peers into battle or getting into trouble with the teachers.’ Felicia chuckles and drags a hand down her face. ‘Connol says she’s lonely.’

‘He wants another?’

A short nod. ‘Yeah. But pregnancy is a terrifying thing. Everything changes: you, your life, the way people treat you. Incredibly invasive.’ She smiles wryly. ‘And this isn’t about Connol, he’s a sweetheart—but suddenly everyone thinks they get a say in how you manage your body, in what your role is, and it’s frustrating.’

He shudders at the thought. Janna knows what Viktor would do if anyone tried to tell him what he should do with this body—a malfunctioning prototype, but his, nonetheless.

‘Don’t have another if you don’t want to, Felicia.’ It seems so simple.

She smiles, melancholic, hand lingering on her tummy. ‘You’re a sweet kid, you know?’

‘Viktor is perfect,’ Silco tuts as he re-enters the room. He passes Viktor a glass of water and two small pills, which he gratefully accepts and gulps down.

Felicia detaches herself from the windowsill. ‘You’ll stay with him, Sil? Got errands to run.’ She winks at Viktor. ‘I hope you feel better soon.’

‘Tell Violet I said hi.’ She gives him finger guns, and the door falls shut.

Silco levels him with a stare. ‘Are you feeling better?’

‘The painkillers need, eh, thirty minutes before they begin to take effect.’

‘Did we wake you?’

‘I woke up from the pain.’

‘You had a dream,’ Silco deduces, crossing his legs. Waiting for elaboration.

Viktor thinks of Silco’s voice coming out of his father’s mouth. His parents, two faceless strangers in the dream world—now that he is conscious, it is a relief to realise he remembers them just fine. The mole on his mother’s cheek; the low rumble of his father’s laugh. There is a proverb, in the Undercity, that says *It takes a village to raise a child.*

He looks away. ‘I don’t remember it.’

‘Really.’ An arched brow. Silco sighs, pinching the bridge of his nose. ‘Dreams are only the manifestation of our worst fears, Viktor.’

He tilts his head. ‘What do you dream about?’

At Silco’s stricken expression, Viktor almost, almost regrets asking, but then he replies, patiently, ‘The river, taking everyone I care about.’ He places a hand on Viktor’s nape, making it impossible to look away. ‘But it will not take you. We won’t allow it.’

There is really nothing to say after that.

*Viktor, nineteen*

Deathly silence falls over Zaun as dust and debris settle over the broken body of the city.

Again, Viktor thinks of homeostasis and the beautiful lie of a living organism self-regulating and adjusting, whatever the external conditions; thinks of the luxury of it. Healing can come from within only if the circumstances allow it; when the body has nothing to feed on, cells start to consume themselves to survive. It is not enough when the body is already thin and weak.

His ears ring with the shouts of protesters and the wails of children and the barks of Enforcers and the throbbing pain of his body and the miraculous beat of his own heart. He is alive—but many are not.

With difficulty, he lowers himself to the rock of the cave, gripping his cane so tightly his knuckles whiten. It is strange; he does not feel a thing. He thinks he will not be feeling things for a while. The cane slips between his hands and clatters on the ground. Every particle attracts every other particle in the universe with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centers of mass, he thinks. Gravity is inevitable like that. So is death and decay. So is the failing of the body. The impossibility of rebellion and change. Axiom, that which commands itself as evident.

The corpses have been removed and buried in the common grave of the water, but no one is scrubbing the blood off the bridge. Viktor thinks he still has copper under his nails and on his hands and up to his elbows where the sleeves are rolled and on his torn clothes and down to his boots—his own blood, his people’s—but he cannot be certain; everything he sees is through red-tinted lenses. There might be blood on his cheek. It might be Felicia’s, perhaps Connol’s. The bodies are all buried together, he cannot test the hypothesis, he cannot compare the blood tests, the results. He doesn’t know; he isn’t sure.

This must be the line so spoken of. This must be the limit they can always extend and push past. The threshold, the point of no return. The road that only keeps on going forward.

Violet and Powder are with Vander; Vander is with Benzo. Viktor is with Silco; Silco, barely alive, barely coherent, is with the doctor. Therefore, Viktor is with the doctor. Has he, too, crossed the line? By making this choice? But there really is no other choice to be made. Vander will understand—Felicia would have understood. But Vander is not Felicia, and Viktor doesn’t know him as well as he knows—as he knew her.

The doctor has not changed at all; it is comforting, though it shouldn’t be, because Silco has changed, and that is terrifying.

‘Vander betrayed us, child. It is just you and I now,’ he says sometimes, when he is not delirious from pain and illness.

And then other times: ‘You are my son, Viktor. I will never forsake you.’

Viktor nods, Silco smiles, and the shrapnel digs in deeper and twists. It is the first time he thinks, *I must leave you*.

Rio’s unmoving body is still connected to those vile tubes, but now she is inside a tank full of green liquid. Viktor limps towards it and lays a hand on the glass; his reflection looks back at him, gaunt and grim. The mutation survives; Rio lives. He used to think if he righted this wrong, he could even out the injustice done to both of them. He understands now that this is impossible.

And other times Silco mumbles about water and thrashes on the operating table and chokes on nothing but his own spite and hatred until he wears himself out and sleeps for days at a time. Viktor bats away the doctor’s hand on his shoulder and stays. Silco wakes and notices Viktor on his bedside and asks him with dead eyes *Have you had enough*—as if it is supposed to *mean* something, only Viktor can’t figure it out, so tears gather in his eyes and tremors run through his body.

‘Don’t cry,’ Silco will say, tenderly, sharply. ‘You’re perfect.’

And Viktor thinks miserably, *I* *cannot leave you*. And hates them both for it.

And when there are other times—

‘Look at me, child.’

A freezing hand grabs his chin and forces his head to the side, when he doesn’t obey immediately. The palm lingers on his cheek, and its touch is soft, then harsh, stinging, and Viktor holds himself so still he might break like glass

—and at some point ‘I must leave you’ becomes: *I* *will leave you*. The inevitability of gravity. The axiom, commanding itself as evident.

Who was it that taught him limits must be pushed and dark doors can be stepped through? *I* *learnt at your knee, father. I cannot be anyone else*.

He thinks of a river diverted to flood the mines. He thinks of ashes washed away and of bodies never identified, features too burned to recognise. With sudden clarity, he realises: *I* *lost* *them both to the water*. Who is there to leave behind? Those who raised Viktor are drowned in the river, one by one, buried beneath the earth.

Evidence supports the claim that it takes a village to raise a child. Evidence has nothing to show for when a village burns. Evidence doesn’t support the claim that all limits must be pushed for science, for revolution. When escalation of demands comes at too high a cost, what is there to do? There are, indeed, some lines that cannot be crossed, after all—this is not a hypothesis, it is a proven fact. Yet they are crossed anyway.

When the opportunity presents itself, it is too easy. Nothing should be that simple in Viktor’s life.

He is walking through the new water facility the Council ordered constructed to pat themselves on the back after the massacre. A way to improve their image in the eyes of the Pilties, as if to say *Look at what we are doing for the trenchers that would rise up against us.* *ungrateful, barbaric*. This is not for the Undercity, not really; it is about vanity, self-assurance, justification.

Loath as he is to admit it, it is still progress.

One of the openings in the wall looks out into the oasis where he used to go. He would like to imagine the sound of Zaunite children playing carrying all the way up here; instead all he can hear is the white noise of water rushing down the wall into the rising mist. Something rustles above his head; Viktor looks up to see foliage growing down from the circular opening, blown by the wind.

The day is fading, and it lights up this small corner of the world in beautiful golden beams. He thinks of the way down to the cave, back to the dark, and pain runs down from his back to his hip to his leg. The hand around his cane spasms. The water falls.

Leave Silco, stay with Silco—it doesn’t need to be a choice. Easier ways out exist. There is the river that has taken so much from him. Viktor peers down, unable to discern where the water ends; perhaps it is mist all the way to the oasis, perhaps it would feel like falling in a cloud. A hypothesis, to be tested. But those are a child’s despairing thoughts, and he stopped being considered a child to the world when he decided to walk into a trinket shop, a pharmacy, a factory, a brothel, looking for work and finding none. He has not been a child for a while now.

He limps away from the ledge, only to find himself stumbling over a yordle.

‘And who might you be?’ The yordle squints his eyes up at him curiously.

Viktor blinks. ‘I was—I must have been lost…’

‘A most fascinating story! How does one find themselves lost up here?’

He scratches his head. ‘Eh—’

A hand patting his back. ‘My dear boy, if you are to sneak around, you need a better excuse. Now, come with me; I promise you are not in trouble.’

‘Who are you, sir?’

A surprised chuckle. ‘Well, that is a question I don’t get often!’ His eyes land on Viktor’s thread-bare clothes, on the cane, on the brace. Viktor squares his shoulders—proud, defensive —but the yordle looks sad, not judgemental. ‘Professor Heimerdinger. And what is your name?’

‘It’s Viktor, Professor.’

*Viktor, twenty-five*

Kind whispers say Viktor is Jayce’s assistant; he cares little for unkind ones. They can think what they will; he has not spent five years topside just to be deterred by petty insults and microaggression. Homeostasis all over again: he is here to right a wrong; he is not here to belong. Viktor doesn’t particularly want to. He’s been called a thief, a liar, an impostor too many times to care what Pilties think of him.

He does not expect Sky, but he is happy to have a fellow Zaunite working alongside him. It is good not to be the only odd one out, good to know someone else cares for the Undercity; Jayce, earnest and well-meaning though he may be, cannot understand, might forget, so Viktor must never. How easy it would be, falling to the rhythm of the work and losing focus for the sake of discovery; Jayce makes everything easier, more chaotic, yet it is the first time Viktor’s mind is quiet. Nothing has ever been so simple before, so he waits for the other shoe to drop.

Only the three of them remain in the lab. Jayce is slumped on the chair, snoring, arm hanging in the air and not touching the floor by a centimetre; Sky, leaning on the desk.

Viktor stands in front of the board, one hand absently coming to touch the curve of his neck. If he introduces a new variable on the calculation, expanding the original equation, and splits the absolute value of x… He scribbles away his thoughts, the chalk making an irritating noise. His eyelids fall; he has to blink several times before he can see what he’s written.

‘Let’s call it a night,’ Sky says, and Viktor startles; she nods towards Jayce. ‘I can help you carry him home.’

‘We would wake him up.’ Jayce’s sleep schedule might match his own, and that is nothing short of miraculous. And depressing. Kindred spirits, Viktor thinks wryly. Partners, Jayce tells everyone who will hear it, all earnest eyes and large gestures. Whispers and laughs.

‘You’re tipping your hand, Viktor.’ Sky’s eyes are sad and serious. ‘Topsiders and trenchers don’t mix well.’

‘He is different.’

‘Jayce is only human. And *you* are only saying that because you’re fond of him.’

His hand stills, falls to his side. ‘You may have a point.’

She spares Jayce a considering look, but when she turns at Viktor, the gemstone casts a blue haze on her face, softening it. ‘He seems quite enamoured with both you and Counselor Medarda. Mingling politics and lust is a dangerous game.’

‘There is no room in the equation for fissure-folk like us, yes? We must be the independent variable.’

She sighs and pushes her hair out of her face. ‘Zaunites are dirt on their boots; it’s offensive, dehumanising, and it’s on purpose. The first time someone learnt I’m from the Undercity, they asked how much I charge for the whole night; can you believe it?’ Sky sounds amused.

‘When I was an undergraduate, a student saw me outside Heimerdinger’s office. They thought I was going to rob him. We had an appointment to discuss my salary as his assistant.’

‘I was invited to this party during my first year—a girl lost her purse, and the first person she accused was me. Her purse had fallen behind a couch. I didn’t even know her name.’

Viktor snorts; Jayce twitches in his sleep, and they freeze. He mumbles something along the lines of *Hextech… golden… beautiful* and goes back to sleep. Viktor exhales and silently nods towards the door. He grabs his cane and limps away from the chalkboard; Sky follows.

They sit outside the lab, in the corridor, dark but for the blue glow peeking through the half-closed door. Viktor stretches his leg on the floor while Sky slides down next to him, hugging her knees to her chest. The Academy is quiet but for their lowered voices. Piltover is serene and polished—nothing like the barking strays of the Underground, the shouting and the swearing.

‘Had any news from home lately?’ Sky asks suddenly.

A dozen letters with handwriting that changed over the last five years: from insane rambling to neat, precise arguments. The delay of an echo is directly proportional to the distance of the reflecting surface from the source and the listener. He is so far away from all he left behind, and yet the repetitive sound doesn’t delay. The words linger in the back of his mind the way they would echo now, in the enclosed and empty room of his childhood. *Come home*.

‘No,’ he says—inquiring, after a pause, ‘Have you heard something?’

‘The Sheriff was murdered in the Undercity; it’s all hushed up, doesn’t make any sense.’

How curious. ‘You would expect them to rise at the opportunity of sending Enforcers, no?’

Wind picks up through the corridor, slamming the lab door shut, snuffing out the blue light, muffling Jayce’s snores. Sky nods; her eyes glimmer in the dark.

‘There’s one more thing.’ She hesitates, but only for a moment. ‘Happened the same day. Vander is dead.’

His heart shrivels like rotten fruit.

Viktor comes home.

Well, first he considers dragging himself all the way down traditionally: crossing the cursed bridge, taking the lift. Then he takes a look at his leg, thinks of how it has been bothering him more than usual, how the cold doesn’t sit right with the tired bones and the weak muscles. Wonders if he will have to make for an untimely exit, if he should preserve his strength.

Viktor takes the bathysphere instead.

Anticipation and anxiety thrum in his nerves; questions plague his thoughts. In what ways has Silco changed, in what ways has he stayed the same? Fear—*will he be happy to see me*— and anger—*does it matter?* When he sees Silco, he can’t be that child again, with his heart blooming at any expression of pride, fondness. Part of Viktor fears he will always carry the boy with him; part of Viktor knows he hasn’t been a child since he left the doctor. These are both true.

Has it really been that long, he wonders when the bathysphere reaches the final stop. It feels like hardly any time has passed, when he follows the routes he used to as a boy, walking through crooked alleys and feeling the strain on his leg. He has not visited since he left, terrified with the two possibilities—that Silco might seek him out, or that he won’t lift a finger to find Viktor; content with knowing that Vander will care for Felicia’s and Connol’s girls and their friends; but if Vander is really gone—and his heart clenches at the thought— Viktor will have to—what? Adopt five children? Bring them topside? What other choice is there?

Some shops have closed, shutters drawn with graffiti over them, and new businesses have emerged; the open-air market buzzes with energy, and traders animatedly argue over their stalls. He ducks into an empty street of a residential neighbourhood; here, the voices grow distant, and buildings lean into one another for support, ropes with hung clothes hovering over his head. If Viktor is macabre enough, he can imagine a body strung up and looming above. There is a sort of quiet in the aftermath of violence, and it feels like that.

Pain is a constant in his life, but Viktor recognises when he is getting tired. Twenty-five is by no means old, but the body’s ability to endure has changed since he was a teenager. It is enough proof that he is not that boy anymore—and also that he needs to sit down soon.

He runs into Sevika shortly after.

‘He’s been expecting you for over a week now,’ she says as she pushes herself off the wall. ‘Doesn’t like being kept waiting.’

‘You’re missing an arm,’ Viktor observes, trying to appear unfazed; the amputation is concealed by a shawl wrapped around her left side. ‘A recent development?’

She scoffs. ‘Got Vander’s brat to thank for it.’

‘*Powder* did this?’

‘A lot has happened since you’ve been gone, Viktor.’

He acknowledges this with a nod. ‘I can make you a prosthetic.’

‘Didn’t ask for one.’

‘Irrelevant,’ he says, lifting a shoulder.

They follow the route towards the Last Drop; his heart is filled with unease, and anxiety mixes with the strain on the lungs. For once the unwillingness to move is more about terror and less about his disability. He clutches his cane and thinks of dichotomies: he longs to see Silco more than anything in the world; he wants to put distance in between them so that his words will stop echoing in his head. A childish hope, because no amount of distance will do.

Down they go, through the Lanes and past the vendors behind their stalls, past the brothels and past Benzo’s shop—blood on the cobblestone, no one bothered to clean it off. Viktor thinks of the bridge connecting their damned city with Piltover and feels sick. His voice won’t work properly to ask what happened, and Sevika offers neither explanation nor kind words. He appreciates it even as terror outplays longing; as he walks forward, it feels more and more like leaving behind whatever he’s accomplished above. He is the boy again, waking up in pain, wanting to be comforted and praised, and Viktor can help it no more than one can help their blood.

The Last Drop appears in their line of sight, and it looks the same, and it feels different. Sevika spares him a glance before she pushes the door open. He follows her inside, and the last part of his armour gives away.

She makes her way through the patrons with practiced ease, and Viktor follows the path she clears. Eyes linger on him, curious, hostile, indifferent, then slide away. A couple of familiar faces—but new ones, too, and not the kind that would frequent the bar when Vander was around. He takes the room in snapshots: conversations all around, clinking glasses, a coin spinning on the table until it falls flat on its side. Booming laughter, money exchanging hands. The stench of alcohol, the claustrophobic feeling of an enclosed, jammed space. They move past it all and head towards the stairs; Viktor looks up at the end of the staircase and considers the merits of giving up before he’s even tried. His leg aches in advance.

Sevika is half-way up before she notices his hesitation. Remembering the cane and the leg, pursing her lips. Silent understanding passes through her expression.

‘I’m sure he will have accommodations next time you visit,’ she says sharply and turns around.

‘Depending on what he’s done,’ he says as he slowly advances up, stronger leg bearing his weight, squeezing his eyes shut through the pain, ‘there may not be a next time.’

Sevika huffs; he can only see the back of her head, top hair pulled back into a small ponytail. ‘Yeah, sure.’

They reach the upper floor, and he exhales shakily. Walking down the corridor, the noise muffled and distant, moving past closed rooms where Violet, Powder and their friends lived. Where are they now, if Vander is gone? What will he say to Jayce if he suddenly acquires five children?

He doesn’t stop at where his room used to be, but his eyes take note of the door out of habit, before he tears them away. Sevika pauses outside Vander’s office and gives him a look that says *Well?*

Hand raised to knock, he abruptly turns.

What he means to say: *Is he recognisable? Has the river taken everything?*

What he says: ‘Is he… changed?’

*Does he despise me now?*

‘You don’t need me to sugargoat it, you never have.’ She jerks her head towards the door. ‘Go see for yourself.’

Two short knocks. A familiar rasp answering, and then Viktor steps into the office and is a boy again.

The room is far darker and sinister than it used to; the furniture is antique, sophisticated, replacing the simple and practical ones Vander had. The window behind the desk has strangely-shaped muntin bars over the green glass, not unlike a butterfly's wings. Silco’s unreadable gaze watches him with resolute focus, pale fingers curled inward before his lips with a cigar he puts out on an ashtray. Viktor wonders, if someone were to put them side to side, would their eyes hold the same intensity?

Silco casually gestures to the chair in front of the desk, and Viktor obliges. He, too, looks different; not dressed in leathers, but wearing a burgundy vest with gold accents over a dress shirt. Collected and precise and nothing at all like the broken vortex of a man Viktor left behind. This Silco is a stranger, and suddenly Viktor is afraid. Perhaps it shows in the hitch of his breath; Silco places a hand on Viktor’s chin and tilts it left and right. The touch is gentle and light; when he lets go Viktor reels back, heart beating loudly in his ears.

‘I thought you might come; are you well, child?’ Silco asks, finally, arm slang over the back of his chair.

Viktor gathers whatever courage he has left and replies, ‘I’m not here to answer inquiries about my health.’

Silco just waits.

‘I am well.’

A bit passes.

‘There are rumours of your breakthrough topside. You make Zaun proud.’

Viktor cocks his head. ‘Do I make you proud?’

‘You have never failed me.’

‘But I left.’ His voice, steady. ‘I’m not sorry for it.’

Silco rises and walks around to lean on the desk. A firm hand on his shoulder. ‘The greatest thing we can do in life is find the power to forgive. Besides,’ and here he looks away, ‘you were a child, and I was… unwell. You did what you had to; I taught you that.’

The hand drops, and Silco detaches himself from the edge of the desk. ‘But this ends now. Take your place by my side. Your room is intact; you will have a lab and funding. Bring the topsider if you have to—I don’t care.’

Viktor shakes his head and stands. ‘Is Vander dead?’

‘Yes.’

‘Was it you?’

Like that child, he still wants to believe the best of Silco. He wants to hear denial, or regret. As an adult, he expects neither.

A strange smile on thin lips. ‘Partially. You remember Felicia’s youngest?’

(Asking Sevika, *Powder did this?)*

He feels sick to his stomach.

‘I gave Vander a choice, and he decided wrong. His children came to the rescue, and the youngest caused an explosion. Her siblings died, Vander was wounded, and he took Shimmer to make one last courageous stand.’

Viktor feels dizzy, grabbing the back of his chair. He thinks of Vander’s heavy hand on his shoulder and a warm smile and how good he was with the children. Vi’s happy face flashes before his eyes, at age two, four, seven; he had watched her grow. He had kept her when her parents were working. And her friends, young, quick to follow her anywhere—*Oh, she’s a* *formidable four-year-old either leading her peers into battle or getting into trouble with the* *teachers*. Felicia, brave and clever and caring and funny and gone, counting on them all to take care of her daughters. And clever, poor Powder, accidentally responsible for an explosion that cost Sevika her arm, for all these deaths?

The dark door, opening wider again; he wants to cross over the threshold. So much unhappiness, so much suffering. Nothing will ever be okay. Ringing in his ears; Silco might be still talking. Viktor forces himself to find a tether and stay in reality.

‘I never blamed you for Felicia. But you insisted on becoming the man Vander thought you were when he tried to kill you.’

‘I only want to help forge the nation of Zaun,’ Silco snaps. ‘No one else is willing to do what it takes, but *I* understand change doesn’t come peacefully.’

‘You’ve done enough.’ Viktor grabs his cane tightly, walking to the door. ‘And to ask of me to return—at best, it is delusional. At worst, it is offensive.’

‘These are necessary choices, child.’ Silco grabs his arm. ‘Don’t look at me like a stranger. You are my son, you *know* me.’

‘You slapped me and cradled my face. You said never leave, but left first. I was scared of you. I didn’t know you then anymore than I do now.’

Viktor wrenches himself away; Silco lets him.

‘Where is Powder?’

‘Why?’

‘Her family is gone. I’m taking her with me.’

For the first time, something akin to cruelty lights up in Silco’s gaze. ‘Are you?’ he asks evenly. ‘See for yourself.’

Down the corridor again, floorboards creaking—six years since he was last here, and still Silco keeps pace with Viktor. They stop outside the room Powder and Vi shared, and Silco, without looking at him, gently pushes the door open to reveal Felicia’s daughter.

She is sitting on the floor, legs tucked beneath her torso as she tinkers with a screwdriver. Her movements are jerky and manic; when she looks up, her eyes are bloodshot and angry.

‘Hello, Powder,’ he says, awkwardly lowering himself to the ground. Silco looms over his shoulder, but Viktor keeps his gaze steady on her.

She flinches hard, head jerking to the side, staring at nothing. ‘It’s *Jinx.*’

He can’t breathe for a moment. Has to swallow his feelings—the surprise, the grief, the protests: every single one. ‘I’m Viktor. I used to hang around the Last Drop and babysit Violet.’

She frowns. ‘Yeah, okay. Why did you leave?’ Accusation paints her voice in ugly swirling colours.

Viktor’s lips twitch with bitter irony. ‘It was necessary; I had to make this choice to follow my own path. What path will you forge? Are you staying with Silco?’

Her hands still on the gadget, then resume their work. ‘He is nice to me,’ Powd—*Jinx* says, eyes darting from Silco to Viktor. ‘Everybody else’s dead.’

Viktor is not fit to raise a child; his role models consist of two dead parents who he misses but doesn’t quite remember, the doctor, Silco—and Felicia. He has to do this for her.

‘You could stay with me. I work in a lab; you like science, no?’

Silco huffs, and his words echo loudly, *Your room is intact; you will have a lab and funding.*

Her eyes widen with child-like wonder. ‘A lab? Like Signed’s? Where do you live?’

‘Eh, topside,’ he says, wincing at how she leans back, horrified, doesn’t pause to wonder who Signed is.

Silco chuckles and crouches down beside him, extends his palm. ‘It’s your choice. You are welcome here.’ He looks unbearably soft, and Viktor wants to wail, and Viktor wants to leave, wants to have never needed to leave.

Jinx doesn’t take his hand, throws herself at him instead. Silco looks at Viktor over her shoulder, arms wrapped around her body awkwardly, then tightly. There is raw emotion behind his good eye.

Muffled by his chest, a tearful request, ‘Maybe I can visit—or, or *you* can?’ Jinx whirls around, still in Silco’s embrace, looking at Viktor with such hope in her eyes. The latter option clearly pleases her more.

Silco’s gaze is fixed on him, waiting for an answer. ‘Again, it’s your choice, child.’

A traitorous thought, *She looks so much like her family.*

He can’t say no, because he has lost the ability to utter words. He gives a small nod, feels a noose around his neck tighten and kick the breath out of his damaged lungs, and the ghost of a smile etches upon Silco’s lips.

When the door closes behind them, he regains his voice, but his anger is a dying fire. Viktor is tired of the mind games and the manipulation. And all this, from one visit. Going home should not be so draining. He wants to travel back to simpler times, when home was home and not a minefield. But what he is chasing is a feeling, not a place, so he has nowhere to go but up. Frivolously, he wants to blame Vander—for nearly killing Silco, for doing something worse instead, for dying.

Viktor limps away, and this time Silco knows to fall back, even after all these years. He fears Silco will always know him, like the way Viktor understands Silco, the way Viktor finds little scraps of his teachings in his behaviour, the way he holds himself proud and undeterred. They move away from the room; only then does he turn to face the man he’s called a father in his head for over a decade.

‘Using Felicia’s child to keep me within your reach?’ he hisses, but the fury is short-lived. ‘It is brilliant, I admit.’

‘I thought you might appreciate the resourcefulness,’ Silco comments, but when Viktor casts him a withering look, he says sternly, ‘I want to keep what remains of this family together, Viktor. For you, for Felicia. For Jinx.’

‘You are selfish.’

A humourless smile. ‘As any parent.’

And now Viktor is trembling whole; ‘I hate you,’ and chokes on the words.

Silco pulls him into an embrace, and Viktor rests his head on his shoulder, hands limp, hanging at his sides. He does not want to want this proximity; he does not want to need it as much as he does. But he always reverts to that boy when it comes to Silco.

‘You should,’ said so softly, ‘you’ve never done anything wrong,’ and Viktor might break.

Per Silco’s command, Sevika escorts him to the bridge. She eyes Viktor strangely as they walk back in silence; his thoughts are too loud, and the world is in a high-pitched, dissonant frequency that gives him a headache.

At the last minute, he recalls his pledge, ‘I will bring blue prints for the prosthetic next time. We can look over them and work out the details, yes?’

She pinches the bridge of her nose and shakes her head. ‘He doesn’t need more liabilities distracting him. You’d be doing both of us a favour if you stayed topside, Viktor.’

He thinks of Powder who calls herself Jinx and shrinks at loud noises. Silco looks at her like he sees something that will shatter him. Viktor doesn’t want to know what his own face does at a simple glimpse of them.

He smiles sadly.

*Viktor, twenty-nine*

The first time he coughs up blood, he doesn’t process the fact until he is washing his hands beneath the lab sink and sees bright red on white tile. His throat has been feeling sore more lately, his lungs heavier, his breath hitching as if there might not be enough air in the world. Still, despite the obvious evidence, he had thought nothing of it, and doesn’t now either. Viktor inspects his hands closely instead, looking for a cut and finding none, with the rising desperation of a man who wants to be proven right.

He catches sight of himself in the mirror and sees the bloodied sleeve on the inside of his elbow where he covered his cough. Dark and heavy bugs beneath his eyes, ashy complexion and a thinness in his looks that fills him with dread. A change has occurred, and he has purposefully missed all its signs, refusing to put the pieces together.

Viktor grabs his crutch and thinks of the cane leaning on the wall of his flat the last few months. Of the brace climbing up his leg like rotten ivy and latching itself from his lower back to his shoulderblades. Of how harder it has become to ignore the worsening ache of his body, when once he could divert his attention elsewhere and simply forget about it; of how extreme pain is a constant now that seems impossible he ever lived without. How he runs himself to the ground and feels it in his hollow bones the way a healthy, young man wouldn’t. How he tires so easily, and his breath wheezes easily, and sleeping is harder because he cannot get comfortable, so he loses sleep—and now he’s coughing up blood.

His first thought is of hatred so intense it surprises Viktor. He hates this malfunctioning body. He hates how it has always failed him, kept him behind and separate, marked him as inadequate, less. Someone to be pitied, not someone that inspires respect and deserves fair treatment. And now, the body will give out soon.

His second thought: fear. How quickly will this deteriorate, and what will it mean for the mind? Viktor has seen far stronger bodies than his succumbing to this sickness, weathering until they are dry shells, devoid of what made them full of life and colour. Turning to Shimmer for comfort, not a cure, because there is none for this. Will it happen to him? Will he lose his dignity and pride, will his systems shut down one by one until he is an atrophied husk? Is this the beginning of the end for everything they have achieved?

Third thought: will Jayce look back at their partnership and remember the man who talked him off the ledge? Or will this memory be replaced by the weak and defective version of himself? This setback will keep them behind in their progress; therefore, he cannot tell Jayce; so to whom can he mention this? Certainly not Silco, certainly not Jinx. It would destroy them, it might drive them to crossing lines Viktor would rather not be responsible for; Janna knows what limits he might be willing to push, just to survive a little longer, to leave something behind, to help the Undercity, before he makes his leave. There is Sky… but she might let something slip in the lab. This won’t do at all.

He turns off the tap and wipes his hands on a towel, inspecting it for bloodstains, relieved when he finds none. His eyes search around the lab for his partner and find Jayce watching him, strange look on his face. Viktor gives him a tight smile and wonders if there is blood on his teeth. He feels around his gums, swallows and feels nauseous at the metallic taste, sick at the thought of Jayce finding out. The coughing fit was not particularly vicious or long, can easily be mistaken for a cold—unless Jayce suspects already, unless he saw the signs Viktor refused to.

Every step is torturous as he limps to the chalkboard; his posture is not quite awful yet, but it will be in a minute, so Viktor carefully deposits himself on the spinning stool with a sharp exhale. Jayce doesn’t need to sit, with broad shoulders and beautiful skin and a perfect existence, he thinks enviously, but only for a moment; then he feels disgusted, horrified. Thank Janna Jayce doesn’t have to worry for his health, that he didn’t know malnutrition in his youth and prejudice in his adulthood, that he didn’t breathe in factory smoke and chemicals, that he didn’t play with dirt and soot. Viktor does not wish this childhood on anyone. He does what he does with his work to help the sons and daughters of Zaun, who grow up in the dark and squint their eyes at the sun, not to condemn Piltovan children to the same fate. It is senselessly cruel, and suddenly another fear strikes him; is this what the sickness of his upbringing will lead him to? He cannot let it consume him until he is utterly changed, full of bitterness and hatred and spite; until he scares those he loves. He doesn’t want Jinx to have to run away because her brother unnerves her.

Viktor hooks his crutch on the chalk edge of the board and spins the stool to face Jayce. ‘Have you made progress with the runes?’

Jayce groans and holds his temples between his thumb and index finger, rubbing the skin. He gestures towards the blackboard with his other hand. ‘I don’t understand what we’re not seeing; it should work, in theory, but…’

‘The object momentarily disappears, then the prototype spits it out.’

‘Exactly.’

Viktor drags the stool closer to the board. He rests his right elbow on the arm folded over his chest, fingers curled in front of him as he considers their calculations. Jayce places a hand over his shoulders without putting real weight; Viktor doesn’t lean back on his side, though he longs to. He would dwell in the space between Jayce’s ribs, if he could. He would cultivate a new, perfect body, if it meant Jayce would love him.

He thinks of Sky’s words—*Topsiders and trenchers don’t mix well*—and Silco’s disdain towards Jayce—*Bring the topsider if you have to,* I *don’t care;* remembers Felicia on the ground, on the bridge, neck twisted at an impossible angle, because Piltover killed her and everyone he cared about. His parents, dead in a mining disaster, working but never reaping the benefits while topside profited. Viktor pauses to wonder if affection for Jayce makes him a traitor to his people; would Silco despise him for it, would Jinx? He quickly realises he doesn’t want to learn the answer to that question, banishing anything not work-related from his mind.

When he doesn’t once have a coughing fit the following months, Viktor allows himself to believe it meant nothing. He doesn’t dwell on it, like he doesn’t pause to think of what Silco is doing to the Undercity, like he doesn’t stop to ponder where Jinx disappears off to. He shuts his ears and eyes to the noise of bomb prototypes, to flashes he catches sight of in Jinx’s lab. There is so much going on with their work—showing progress for the sake of not losing their funding, figuring out what they have gotten wrong, why theory doesn’t translate into practice—that he loses time, stays up late, sleeps in the lab; then wakes, muscles spasming. Viktor learns the new limits of the body again, vomits and remembers being a boy and waking up somewhat like this, with water on his bedside, painkillers neatly arranged on a white plate, a cool hand on his forehead, *Did we wake you?* But he can never go back to a place that exists only in his memory.

He delays visiting Zaun for the sole purpose of avoiding Silco’s piercing eyes. Viktor always folds eventually in front of him, so he has to keep away now. Silco truly believes it when he says Viktor’s perfect—except Viktor isn’t, so he feels like a fraud, not worthy of this unwavering certainty. And though he doesn’t come home, he expects he will be getting a visitor soon. Jinx hates Piltover more than the average Zaunite; therefore, it is a testament to her determination that, as soon as a week passes, she takes it upon herself to see Viktor.

It is uncanny to wake up with her face inches away from his; when she sees his eyes open she grins, ‘Boo!’

She is perched on the desk he is slumped on, legs kicking in the air. Viktor raises his disobedient body from the inconvenient position he had fallen asleep in, aware she is observing his slow, lethargic moves. The moment he turns, she hastily looks away. Twists her long braid around her arm and watches the hair unfold and fall when she lets the end of the braid go.

Abruptly, she thrusts an open notebook in front of his bleary eyes. ‘You fell asleep on your notes, silly. Good news is I took care of them for you.’

Viktor blinks, thinks he can make out a neon-blue doodle on the corner of the page.

‘Thank you, Jinx.’ He takes the notebook, rubs his eyes and wonders exactly how bad he looks; is it terminally-ill levels of bad, or regular-Viktor levels of bad? He snorts at his own joke, and Jinx gives him a sideways look he interprets as *You are so old and weird.*

Jinx grabs his safety goggles and inspects them closely; makes a non-committal sound and throws them behind her with a clutter. ‘You missed your monthly appointment, Persephone, so the big scary baby flipped a table and sent reinforcements—me—to make sure the Pilties aren’t keeping you hostage.’

‘Did he really?’ Viktor asks wryly; what an image, Silco flipping a table.

Jinx barks out a laugh. ‘Nope. But he was getting all antsy and stuff, pissing me off, so,’ she hops off the desk and puts her hands on her hips, ‘here I am, to save you from the baaad topsiders.’

He blinks.

Her shoulders drop. ‘You aren’t happy to see me.’

‘I’m overjoyed,’ he says, simply.

She scoffs, blowing away strands of hair from her face, but he sees her small smile. Gestures like these remind him how much younger she is, why she must be protected, why he cannot leave her alone. Janna knows how Viktor would have turned out if he had made a coward’s choice all these years ago and didn’t leave. He wants to tell her that sometimes staying away is the only option, that sometimes running is not a bad thing, is not an axiom, commanding itself as evident. That Silco is not the man she thinks, but she knows already—and then, comically, the urge to defend him arises, how he was not always as he is now, you know, which is stupid; this is the only Silco Jinx has ever known. Does she even remember that Felicia was his friend? Does Silco? Does it matter?

‘Come,’ he says and rises, grabbing his crutch and reaching for the keys hanging from the wall. ‘We will lock up and go home.’

‘Where to, o glorious leader?’

‘To my flat, my favourite explorer.’

Her amusement evaporates. ‘Not the Undercity?’

He shakes his head, and she groans but trails behind. At first she walks in exaggerated and staggering steps, then abruptly catches up as Viktor steps outside the lab; he holds the door open as she bolts out, braids swinging.

Each move is exhausting; Viktor keeps expecting to break, to reach the limit, but then keeps on going, crossing the threshold every time he thinks Now the body will give up; now the legs will fail. Now he has had enough. Silco says they are perfect; Viktor feels on the verge of shattering. He thinks he might prefer crumbling right now, right here, instead of going through this waiting stage of uncertainty—but no, not in front of her.

‘You’re walking weird,’ Jinx comments as she skips ahead to walk backwards, footsteps echoing in the empty corridor; at his lightly arched eyebrow she amends, ‘Like, weirder than usual.’

‘That’s still rude,’ Viktor says evenly, too tired for anger. Silco sets no boundaries, rewards all behaviours, will clean up any mess for her. It’s not Jinx’s fault.

She winces, one hand grasping the elbow of her opposite arm. ‘Sorry.’

They leave the Academy building behind. Night has truly fallen by now, and the sky-scrapers reaching out into the sky are lit up, a poor substitute for stars. There are none to be seen, of course, neither from Piltover nor from the depths of the Undercity, where even sunlight sometimes fails to make it through fumes leaking from corroded pipework and through the Grey. The air topside is almost artificially clear in comparison, the streets deceptively polished, as if to hide behind the stench of the upper class, the corruption, the tight-lipped pleasantries. Fortunately, Viktor is not subjected to such nonsensical exchanges; unfortunately, when his existence is acknowledged, he is subjected to much worse. In every hitched breath he draws, he must be reminded that he doesn’t belong; in every broken step he takes, of how grateful he should be. It is endlessly, amusingly paradoxical. If he has no place here, why should he thank them for being accepting? If he bears the damage they caused, why do anything at all?

In the dark, Piltovan surreality is easier to swallow, its crass citizens holed up in their fancy residences, its extravagant lifestyle not so obvious, but dim, weakened. Zaun thrives at these twilight hours where the line between what’s real and what’s fantasy tilts and turns kaleidoscope, where neon colours cut through the smog and give the polluted dust a dream-like quality. During those moments, if one suspends their disbelief, they can believe in magic ancient and arcane, the kind of superstition created by the desperate searching for meaning in suffering when there is none to be found. Satisfaction exists in the fact that when the sun falls down the sky and disappears, the Underground outshines Piltover in a way coal shouldn’t be able to outshine a precious jewel.

‘Are you mad?’ Jinx suddenly asks.

‘About what?’

‘I said I’m sorry.’ Her tone is somewhere between defensive and petulant, eyes darting from one spot to another, seeing, he suspects, a thousand things at a time. ‘Silco’s brooding, so I come here; but you’re also brooding.’

‘I’m thinking.’

She moves in front of Viktor, who pauses so he doesn’t bump into her.

‘Whatcha thinking about?’ she asks, squinting one eye and peering up at him through…

‘Are those Silco’s eye drops?’

‘What, this old thing?’ She dangles the injection syringe in front of her face like the pendulum of a clock moving back and forth, then makes it take off as if it is an airship. When he tries to grab the syringe, she hides it behind her back. ‘Definitely not.’

‘He needs these.’ He sighs, drumming his fingers on his crutch.

‘Too bad we’re not heading home,’ she says cheekily, pretending to wipe an imaginary tear away as Viktor starts walking again. ‘Guess someone should’ve thought of that sooner.’

‘I changed my mind. We are going to the Last Drop,’ he decides—Jinx punches the air in delight, falling into step— ‘and *you* are explaining to him why you have these.’

‘Wait, what?’

Viktor cackles, half lung damage and half mirth.

When the lab door opens, Viktor doesn’t look up from the hexgate prototype. From his peripheral vision he deduces it to be Jayce, hypothesis proven right when a warm hand lands on his shoulder. No one else gets close enough topside but for Heimerdinger, whose short stature makes him easily recognisable, and Sky, who isn’t working today.

‘Hey, you’re here already,’ Jayce says and drops on the chair beside him. ‘I’m an early riser, yet you always beat me getting here first. Do you ever sleep?’

‘Of course not,’ Viktor replies smoothly, ‘I’m fueled solely by sweetmilk.’

Jayce chuckles—strange, Viktor was not trying to be funny. ‘Which reminds me, I restocked your sweetmilk supplies.’

Now Viktor does look up to find Jayce smiling. ‘You did? That is very kind.’

‘Want some? Pass me your mug.’

Jayce leaves his side for a moment, and Viktor watches him open the lab fridge and take a new bottle of sweetmilk out, filling the mug. It sets such a domestic picture, unleashes a new tide of yearning for the unavailable, renders him immobile with affection and bitter guilt. *You* *betray everything we stand for*, Silco whispers, cradling his face; *You’re leaving me for him,* Jinx spits, hitting his arm. Viktor feels the sharp, permanent sting in the failing heart that has little to do with his health, feels like he has to hold himself back and not allow the fondness to fester into something as unforgivable as love.

He inclines his head in thanks, fingers numb as they close around the handle.

‘The doodles aren’t your design.’

‘Doodles?’ He takes a good look at the front of the mug; there is a neon pink monkey drawn on it. ‘Ah. My sister’s.’

‘You have a *sister?’*

‘Eh, not of the biological kind. A childhood friend, not unlike Ms Kirammann.’

‘Sometimes it feels like I don’t know anything about you,’ Jayce blurts out, ‘which is fine, we’re partners—but you’re my best friend, so is it that you’re private? Or am I doing something?’

Strange. ‘Where is this coming from?’

‘You’re evasive all the time.’

Bewildered, ‘No, I’m not.’

‘But you are, Viktor,’ Jayce exclaims, frustration seeping through his voice as he counts on his fingers. ‘You were ill last week, and I know you didn’t want me to know; you disappear, and I’ve no idea where you’re going—and now you have a sister I’ve never heard of before.’

He wants to say *You know* *me*. Then thinks, with dread, of a father who once said the same words; of an angry nineteen-year-old child uttering the accusation that rings true to this day, *I* *didn’t know you then anymore than I do now*. Of wondering what would show, if one were to place them next to each other and say, *Now point out* *the similarities*. Guilt gnaws at him; has he, in the quest to be less like Silco, become more like him?

Viktor reaches out to… do what exactly? His hand falls on his thigh. ‘I didn’t want you worrying over a cold. When I disappear, as you say, I’m visiting family in Zaun; my sister’s name is Jinx. I don’t mean to shut you off. You are very dear to me.’

Jayce stares at his feet and gives a small nod; ‘I will worry anyway. And you don’t have to hide that you’re going to—Zaun. I would never think less of you for any of it.’

He lifts a shoulder, though his heart is loud in his ears. ‘Eh, many would.’

‘Well, *I* wouldn’t. You’re smart, you’re sensitive, kind—even when others have been nothing but cruel and prejudiced in return.’ Jayce smiles. ‘I admit I admire that.’

‘It is sweet to think so, Jayce,’ Viktor says, because he doesn’t know how to handle any of that.

‘It’s the truth,’ Jayce says fiercely and, after a moment’s hesitation, embraces him. Viktor holds his breath and stays still, but Jayce bats his arm, ‘Hug back, we’re friends,’ so he lets himself melt in the comfort, lets the affection fester into more, attach itself to his hollow bones, steal the breath from his lungs and replace it with something untainted, terrifying, unforgivable, and true.

He does not begrudge Silco his trauma, but he resents the way it manifests in his behaviour, how it shapes—no, limits Silco’s view of his world, of Viktor, of Jinx. Viktor has to make peace with the fact that there are no terms and conditions in this thing with Jayce—no paranoid fear of betrayal and abandonment, no holding a dead friend’s child over his head as a weapon to force him to stay. He has to make peace with how devastating that is.

*Viktor, thirty-two*

He is getting worse.

Most of his days consist of working and hurting; they blur into suffering that is never-ending, doesn’t stop when he closes his eyes, but seeps through his dreams like poison in the blood. Nightmares he wakes up from sweating and in pain. Disturbing dreams of choking in blood, of those dear to him finding out, of the rot in his lungs latching into his insides, climbing up his throat, infectious. Sleep is meant to be regulated by homeostatic and circadian processes, yet the sick organism of the body fails in such fundamental ways that he cannot keep up with the deterioration, with the erasure of what shapes the person. Viktor can feel himself fading, can feel aspects of him collapse in front of the weight of the erosion, so tired that putting effort into being human seems unachievable, unbearable. He is a dying version, turned inside out, veins showing, nerves so tightly strung they are coming apart. His flesh might open up, unleash the damage to the prim and polished world of Piltover. Every time he breathes, the air feels polluted; every time he coughs, people distance themselves with unease, and he does not hate them, for who would approach a roadkill?

And Jayce, sweet and righteous and awkward, is not here. A bitter thought: He’s off playing politics, away from their dream. Traitor, traitor.

When he doesn’t make the regular visit to Zaun, not because Viktor wants to stay away from Silco and Jinx, but because he physically cannot walk that day—and he wants to, he wants to, because despite their falling out, a cool hand will touch his forehead and not bat an eye at his burning fever and say *It’s* *alright now, child,* and Viktor will see the tilt of his head and think of his mother, and hear the low timbre of his voice and think of his father—when he doesn’t make the regular visit to Zaun, he expects Jinx to come with guns blazing. Yet she is nowhere to be found, and Jayce is nowhere to be found, and Sky is kind, but she is not who he wants by his side now, not for this.

Though he shouldn’t, though he should rest, he turns to their work, as long as his brain is functioning—for how much longer—searching for solutions to the unsolvable problem of all humanity: death. The answer is hidden somewhere in the runes, in the hexcore, in the arcane, in superstition deeply embedded in his culture, his roots—desperate people believe in magic, and he is desperate, dying; who can begrudge him this?

Heimerdinger does. When Jayce is finally there, Heimerdinger sees the potential and shies away, deems the Hexcore too dangerous, accuses him of changing—as if Viktor has another choice, another way of keeping himself alive, as if the trauma of the sickness isn’t so heavy that he feels himself retreat further back in his shell. He thinks he might understand Silco now in a way he could not before. Nothing feels right or wrong anymore. Lessons learnt at the feet of teachers he came to despise resurface and swirl inside his mind: Silco’s smooth words—*There is no cost too high for this cause, child*—the doctor’s gravely voice—*There is* *no limit to what science can achieve, and therefore no limit to what a scientist can do.* If it keeps him alive, what isn’t he willing to do?

He seeks out the doctor; he understands now.

Rio looks as dead as he feels. Viktor fixes his gaze on her tank and sees his reflection through the green tint—how healthy it appears, how perfect. He extends a shaking hand, flat on the cold glass behind which Rio’s body floats; imagines feeling her trembling heartbeat, imagines himself in her place, purple running through the veins and the nerves, trapped forever but miraculously, impossibly alive. This would not just benefit him, but the whole of the Undercity, the children plagued by the terrible cough, the lurking sickness. He cannot prevent the cause, but he could cure the aftermath, and to do that, he would have to be alive, and to do that—

‘If you take this path,’ the doctor says—no, warns, holding the vial, ‘they will despise you. Love and legacy are the sacrifices we make for progress.’

Viktor thinks of love and how it festers into something unforgivable not unlike sickness; takes the Shimmer, says, ‘Jayce will understand.’

Jayce does not understand.

A riot rages on the bridge, and he feels nauseous just at the thought. The atmosphere is thick with dust and tear gas, making him blink rapidly and cough into a bloodied handkerchief. The Sheriff said something about a blockade, but Viktor’s ears are ringing from the noise, and he does not pay attention to Enforcers. Now he sits and waits, like some chastised, misbehaving child—how condescending, how humiliating.

A ripple of air, and he doubles over again, choking on his own blood.

Then Jayce arrives; large gestures and the furrow of his brows. ‘You didn’t say they were from the Undercity!’

It surprises him, the sting of it like a slap. He feels not unlike a child, wandering in the dark and afraid, waiting for the end. Waiting for the parents that will never come home again. Waiting for the man Silco used to be—kinder, softer—to return. Waiting for that earnest and lovely boy Jayce once was. Two thoughts cross his mind:

*I thought you understood*.

*Come back*.

‘What difference does that make?’ Vktor says—stupid, he has been so stupid and so blind and so naive.

‘What diff—they’re dangerous!’ Righteous anger in this beautiful, hazel, stranger’s eyes, the kind of which in powerful men can mean nothing, nothing good. And nowadays Jayce holds so much power.

‘*I* am from the Undercity,’ Viktor says coolly.

He remembers a conversation from a lifetime ago, the hope, the unshakeable belief; Jayce is different. A hypothesis after all—not an axiom, as he had thought—just waiting to be proven wrong. Viktor remembers: *I would never think less of you.*

*Liar*.

Is this what Silco felt when Vander betrayed him?

He wakes up suddenly, wheezing and terrified and wondering *Is it time?*

But it’s Jinx looking into his face, her weight on his chest, knees digging into his ribs. His lungs constrict as Viktor weakly tries and fails to push her off, scared because it’s the middle of the night, and she looks murderous, and she won’t move even though he can barely breathe. He falls back on the mattress, and it knocks the air out of him again. He struggles to inhale; his breath keeps getting caught in his throat. Cold fear running down his spine; Jinx looks deranged, and she might kill him, and then she will never forgive herself.

‘Did you know?’ Jinx spits out viciously, ‘did he tell you?’

He shakes his head, though he has no idea what she is referring to. Viktor feels the deadly thing always latched in his lungs climb up the trachea and up the throat; he coughs again, and this time it doesn’t stop, and his body seizes with the force of it, in pain, in terror.

Blood falls on Jinx’s cheek, and she wipes it, looking at her raised hand with confusion. She rolls away from Viktor, lands on the side of the bed with a thump, waiting out the fit; her face is covered in shadow, strange, unreadable. The weight on his lungs lifted, he takes in ragged breaths and looks at her, this girl he calls his sister, this girl who almost killed him. Viktor rarely ever thinks of Violet, but now he does; she comes in his mind, forever younger than Jinx is now, fresh with the agony of losing her family, realising Powder caused the explosion in the old warehouse. They were just children.

Jinx twists the sheets around her finger; lets the fabric drop to clutch her head; ‘She’s back from the dead just as you’re about to join them,’ laughs hysterically, eyes darting from one point to another; hits her head with her fist. ‘Shut up, shut up. Why’s everybody leaving?’

‘I can’t help it this time,’ he says, apologetic, sitting up cautiously. He leans his back on the bed frame and holds himself still, waiting out the pain, though it will never stop.

Jinx moves to reach out; her hand pauses mid-action, fingers curling into her palm, hand falling on her thigh instead. She wraps her arms around herself. ‘You left once.’

Viktor considers this. After the riot on the bridge, after Felicia’s death, he had gone with Heimerdinger and not looked back until Vander’s death. They had not been close, Powder and he; Viktor used to babysit Violet, not her. The accusation rings unfair. But desperate people will latch onto anything, even a monster, even a lie, so he nods; he understands.

‘Yes. But not once ever since.’ Something nudging his thoughts. ‘Jinx, what did you say? About someone back from the dead?’

She huffs. ‘You really don’t know; Vi is alive. Big baby kept the secret to himself.’ A hollow turning of her lips upwards; her head tips towards her shoulder as if the strings holding it upright had been cut, braids falling to her side. ‘So much for being the prodigal son, huh. He didn’t trust you.’

‘I don’t understand,’ Viktor says, helpless, hurt.

Jinx giggles. ‘You will, soon. Promise.’

Something clutters on the floor—one of her monkey bombs; his eyes widen—exploding in blue colour; she’s gone when the air clears. He coughs again, leans over the bed and throws up. In the bathroom, he washes his mouth and spits blood on the sink. When Viktor catches sight of himself in the mirror, the man meeting his gaze looks haunted. He turns the light off.

He wakes up before the sunrise to prepare for the day Jayce will present the peace treaty to the Council, when he hears heavy banging on the door.

‘Boss sent for you,’ Sevika grits out, and he frowns because she never calls Silco that. Her fists clench at her sides, the prosthetic making a metallic noise; she is none too pleased to act as the messenger, and it shows.

‘Why?’ he asks, feigning indifference. His hand tightens around the crutch. Jayce has avoided telling the specifics of the negotiations to Viktor. Could Silco want to talk about it? A logical conclusion, and yet…

‘The girl was injured. Might not make it.’

He can’t breathe for a long moment, has to lean all of his weight on the crutch and stare at nothing, has to just think. But his mind is a fog of guilt and horror and grief so heavy he cannot shoulder it.

‘Who is tending to her?’ Voice hoarse and alien.

‘You won’t like it; Signed, the doctor.’

The image flashes behind his eyes clearly: Rio floating in a huge tank, held by wires coming out of her skin. Jinx had mentioned Signed, once, but Viktor hadn’t known… so many things, after all.

He gives a terse nod and follows her out, through the city, through the debris on the ruined bridge.

Viktor comes home.

Or at least he comes back to Zaun, to Silco, to the doctor and the cave and Rio. Is this where home is? Somewhere along the way it became a small dot in the narrow horizon of his sight, until he blinked, and it wasn’t there anymore—a star gone out, a feeling, a memory, not a place. Somewhere along the way he lost sight of what ia meant.

Jinx is strapped on the operating table, unmoving but for the light rise and fall of her chest, the sudden twitching of her fingers before they lax. Her veins shine purple and fade, until her body spasms again. The doctor is counting her heartbeat, examining her eyes. Silco is slumped on a chair, covered in the crystal shadow of the cave, so serene and so unlike himself; when Viktor approaches him, he doesn’t wake. Sevika whirls around, at the doctor.

‘What did you do?’ she sneers, bunching his shirt with a fist and lifting him close to her face.

‘He couldn’t bear to see her in pain,’ the doctor says lightly. ‘He will wake in a few hours.’

Sevika shoves him back, but her anger seems to die out; she turns to Viktor. ‘I have to go. Who knows what might happen while he—’ jerks her head towards SIlco— ‘is out of the picture?’

He nods, barely registering her retreating footsteps. Viktor limps to Jinx’s side, opposite of the doctor, and looks—at her dirtied face, the blood between her teeth, sliding down her chin, the dust on her hair, her clothes. She twitches and gasps, eyes opening but seeing a million miles away, back arching, straining against the straps, then suddenly loses all of her energy and falls on the table.

‘Shimmer… did this?’ he says, slowly; can’t quite wrap his head around it.

He feels the doctor’s eyes on him over Jinx’s body, doesn’t meet them.

‘Please sit down, Viktor. We cannot know if she will survive the procedure.’

He drops himself on the chair; his body is a heavy thing, anchoring him to the bottom of the steel oasis of his childhood. Polluted and murky, nearly dried now.

Jinx shifts on the table, fists clenching, fighting against the restraints. His sister, almost dead —for what? To satisfy Silco’s brutality? To build the weapons Viktor refused to? Is this the line meant to be crossed? The limit to be pushed; the threshold, a step through the dark door? And Shimmer, coursing through Jinx’s veins, causing her such pain, such damage; she is strong, but at what cost will she make it? How could this cure ever be worth the life it would leave behind? This cursed drug, polluting the streets of the Undercity, and now this supposed variant would latch itself on his sister’s insides and leave behind dirty fingerprints. And yet he desperately wishes that she survives this, however high the cost; and yet he knows now he can never take the Shimmer for himself.

And Jinx, what had she been trying to prove? Stealing the gemstone, as if Viktor wouldn’t have given her one to experiment with, if only she had asked, if only he had known. If only he had never turned away. If only neither cared what Silco thought of them. His heart constricts painfully. *My sister*, he thinks; *she is my sister*. Looks: at Signed, looming over her, tending to her wounds with poison; at Silco, unconscious on a chair because he could not bear to see what he had caused. Thinks, And the man responsible for this is my father. He is no longer sure father means the doctor or Silco.

A sharp, minimal sting on the side of his neck—he nearly misses it, but reality tilts, and his head is so heavy it drops on his chest, and the doctor is saying something, but Viktor is submerged beneath water, can hear nothing but the silence of every problem in the world fading away.

The eyes can’t quite open properly, so he observes it all through blurry, narrowed sight. The chin, tucked on the chest; the head too heavy to move; the rise and fall of the chest too slow. The lungs refuse to expand when he inhales, refuse the Undercity air, only constrict, only make him wheeze out weak breathing. This cannot be my body, he thinks with the echo of panic. But this has been his body as long as he can recall.

Cold hands harshly grab his jaw and raise his head—Silco. He looks furious; he looks terrified. Viktor tries to smile because even as a boy he had wanted to please his father, but it feels like a grimace on his face, muscles numb and unmovable. Silco lets go, lips moving but making no sense, and Viktor’s head sags, not before he catches sight of the operating table: empty. This means something, but he cannot pinpoint what.

Out of frame, the doctor explaining: ‘He exhausted himself coming here. But I miscalculated the extent of the sickness; he should have shaken off the drug by now.’

Urgent: ‘Help him. Fix it. You did it with her.’

‘I warn you, it might not be the same. The boy has been dying for a while.’

Silco’s deathly pale face contorting—first in anguish, then in anger. ‘Do it.’

(*Suddenly everyone thinks they get a say in how you manage your body*; Felicia’s melancholic smile, a childhood ago.)

Shining purple on a syringe—no, no, he doesn’t want it. The needle near, then piercing the skin—alien and cold, the body recognises it as invasive, unwanted. He thrashes against the unyielding hold on his shoulders, but his moves are sluggish and slow. When excruciating pain comes, there is no resisting anymore, only wishing they’d let him die instead.

Someone behind him, just out of his peripheral vision; the eyes latch on their familiar shadow reflected in front of him. Broad shoulders, larger than life: Jayce. He tries to move, but his feet are rooted to the ground—*Can’t leave now*, Jinx giggles, fades into Silco’s sneer.

Jayce circles around his chair and crouches in front of him: head tilted, reaching to hold his hands between them. *I’m afraid this will be quite* *painful*. Voice hoarse and cold and unlike Jayce’s; the beloved face morphs into pale, damaged features. The face is the doctor is Jayce is Silco is asking him to change, leaving him no choice.

A child, angrily tagging at his trousers, *Why did* *you* *leave?* She has Violet’s hair and Powder’s eyes, and they both look like Felicia so much that he never, never told them.

A silhouette on the wall, the outlines of the long coat, the way he holds himself; Silco turning just for a moment to look at him—the hooked nose, the sharp edges of his face—then turning into the pitch black of the mine’s adit. Off to join the dead of his life.

It is only a hallucination, but Viktor starts screaming.

Waking up, the world has changed definition in his eyes; every detail of sound or smell is too sharp and clear, when all there is to see is grey and dirt. His head is throbbing to the same rhythmic pattern coursing through his veins, to the same beat of his heart. He is breathing too quickly, and it should trigger a coughing fit, one he shouldn’t survive, but instead, Viktor feels—for the first time after months—that a simple inhale, exhale won’t break him. Feels at once aware and unaware, in the sense that everything surrounding him is too loud and precise, while the ache of the muscles and lungs has quietened down. It is still there, a slight catch of breath Viktor will always carry, the inescapable chronic pain, but the sickness is like an animal dying on the side of the road.

He is, it appears, healthier than he has been in years. But when he raises his hands in front of him, he sees purple lining following the curve of his veins on the back of his palm, remembers struggling against an unrelenting grip of someone who hasn’t let him go from the moment a young boy met a tired young man. Is it Silco, is it the doctor? He knows it doesn’t matter. Shimmer is in his blood; the line has been crossed.

‘He should’ve let us both die, doncha think, big brother.’

He flinches and looks up to see Jinx leaning on the cave’s opening. Her eyes are purple instead of blue; she looks drained out and numb. Her nails scratch at the elbow of the opposite arm in auto-pilot.

‘Jinx,’ he breathes out, relief seeping through his hoarse voice, ‘you’re alive.’ He stands on unsteady feet and has to grab the back of the chair to stabilise himself, before teaching for his cane. ‘Where is Silco?’

Some unnameable emotion building up behind her eyes; the corners of her lips quirking up, smile empty. ‘Lemme show you, silly.’

It feels like she is playing a version of herself, forcing that which is no longer there. Yet by the time they reach the abandoned warehouse, Viktor already understands and wills himself to be more forgiving to his sister at thirty-two than a grieving Violet could have been at fifteen.

He thinks of: a girl—Powder—age eleven, a smoking warehouse, dead bodies of friends and of a father.

They were children, all of them. Jinx wasn’t Violet’s fault. Vander shouldn’t have been Powder’s irredeemable mistake. Leaving was the only option for Viktor then.

He drags himself to the scene of the crime, Jinx on his side.

‘We should take him to the river,’ she says, tonelessly. She might be suggesting they have sweetmilk with their breakfast.

‘Let the water take him one last time from us.’ He could be observing that the variable from their equation was forgotten three sets of parentheses before. Viktor feels very old, very tired.

*How can I make peace with what you did now?* Silco is dead, and his legacy isn’t the nation of Zaun, but damage. A death so sudden that Viktor feels utterly unprepared; thinks, childishly, that this is unfair.

He dreams of the adit, of the tall man with the long coat—Silco—of a young girl—Powder— reaching up and taking his hand. They walk towards the dark together. The scene cuts to an asphodel on the windowsill of his childhood home; a rush of wind rises from the mine, and the flower wavers.
