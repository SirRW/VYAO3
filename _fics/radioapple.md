## Chapter 1: The King Receives

Lucifer arrived twenty minutes early, three signs under his arm and the kind of bright, forced cheer he wore whenever he decided he was going to be useful. The hotel’s new façade still smelled of fresh paint and ozone. He set the signs carefully against the steps so Charlie would see them the moment she came out.

The first was a towering thing of gold leaf and black enamel, the words *Hazbin Hotel – Welcome, Sinners* arched in a typeface that belonged on a royal proclamation. The second was pure carnival: red and white stripes, a cartoon apple grinning from one corner, letters that practically shouted. The third was smaller, hand-lettered on a piece of pale wood he’d sanded himself. The paint had bled a little at the edges of the *H*. He’d almost thrown it out twice, then decided the imperfection made it look like something a father might actually make.

He adjusted the handmade one so it sat a fraction forward of the others and told himself she would choose it.

The street was not empty.

A line of demons stretched from the front doors, around the corner, and out of sight. Some held papers. Others held cameras. A few held what looked suspiciously like gift baskets. Lucifer’s first thought was pure, startled delight. Charlie had done it. People had come. He straightened his coat, practiced a smile that tried for regal and landed somewhere near enthusiastic, gathered the three signs back under his arm, and started toward the doors.

A lanky soul with too many elbows stepped out of line and thrust a scroll at him.

“Your Majesty—petition for private audience with the Crown. I’ve been waiting three hours. Where do I—”

Lucifer blinked. “I’m just here for the open house.”

The soul looked at him as if he’d claimed to be a houseplant. “The radio said the King of Hell would receive every visitor personally. I’ve got a grievance against the Overlords and a very reasonable request for a small duchy—”

Someone else called, “Is there a form for marriage licenses signed by the Morningstar?”

A third voice, farther back: “Does the personal reception include a photo opportunity or is that extra?”

Lucifer’s smile stayed frozen while the rest of his face tried to catch up. He pushed through the doors, signs still tucked against his side.

The lobby was already crowded. Vaggie stood behind a makeshift reception desk, wings half-raised, looking like she was one more request away from violence. When she saw him she exhaled hard enough to ruffle the papers in front of her.

“Oh good. You’re here. Maybe you can explain this.” She held up a printed transcript of Alastor’s morning broadcast. Lucifer skimmed. The Radio Demon’s voice was easy to hear even on paper—bright, delighted, just a little too pleased with itself. *…and as a special treat for all prospective guests, His Majesty the King of Hell has graciously agreed to receive each and every one of you in person today. What an opportunity!*

A second sheet listed the questions already asked: formal petitions, requests for royal blessing on business ventures, one particularly optimistic proposal of marriage to the Princess, and three separate demands that the King settle long-standing family curses “while he’s free.”

Lucifer’s ears felt hot. He folded the paper very carefully.

Alastor was leaning against the far end of the desk, cane hooked over one wrist, smile already in place as if he’d been waiting for the exact second Lucifer finished reading.

“Ah, Your Majesty. Fashionably early. Wonderful turnout, isn’t it? Charlie’s flyers were charming, of course, but a little… optimistic. I thought a personal touch might help fill the seats.”

Lucifer kept his voice light. “You thought announcing that the King of Hell was taking walk-ins would help.”

“And look.” Alastor gestured at the lobby with a flourish. “It did.”

“There’s a difference,” Lucifer said, “between attracting people who might stay the night and attracting people who want something from the Crown. One of those is a guest. The other is a problem you just invited through the front door.”

Alastor’s smile didn’t flicker. “A full lobby is still a full lobby.”

Lucifer opened his mouth. Charlie came through the side door before anything useful could come out of it.

She was already talking, already glowing with the kind of hope that made Lucifer’s chest ache. “Dad! You brought the signs—oh, there are so many people, can you believe it—” She stopped when she saw his face, then the papers in Vaggie’s hand. Her expression flickered.

Lucifer held out the handmade sign like a peace offering. “I made a few options. This one’s… smaller. I thought maybe for the front desk.”

Charlie took it carefully. Her fingers traced the uneven lettering. For a second the noise of the lobby dropped away and she looked at him the way she had when she was small and he still knew how to be the center of her world. “It’s perfect,” she said, soft. “Thank you.”

Alastor stepped in before the moment could settle. “Isn’t it? And how fortunate His Majesty could join us after all. I took the liberty of letting everyone know the King would be available. Such a rare treat. One so seldom has the chance to count on him for these little appearances.” The words were warm, almost fond. Anyone half-listening would have heard only courtesy. Lucifer heard the rest of it—every missed recital, every empty seat, every year Charlie had learned not to expect him—delivered with a smile that never slipped.

Charlie’s shoulders tightened. She looked from Alastor to Lucifer and back, already reaching for the blame like it was hers to carry. “Dad, I’m so sorry—I didn’t ask him to say that, I didn’t know he was going to—”

The sharper reply sat ready on Lucifer’s tongue, aimed squarely at Alastor. He looked at Charlie instead—at the way she was already trying to shoulder a mess that wasn’t hers—and the words stayed where they were.

“Hey.” Lucifer kept his voice low enough that the nearest demons had to lean in if they wanted to hear. “I know whose idea this was. Not yours.” He tipped his head toward Alastor without looking at him. “You wanted an open house. You still do?”

Charlie’s shoulders loosened a fraction. She glanced at the handmade sign still in her hands, then at the crowd pressing toward the desk. “I still want them to see the hotel. The real hotel. Not… whatever they think they’re here for.”

“Then that’s what they’ll get.”

Lucifer stepped up onto the low riser that had once been part of the reception desk, signs tucked under one arm. The lobby noise dipped. A few hopeful faces turned toward him like flowers tracking the sun.

“Good afternoon,” he said, bright and carrying. “I am, in fact, the King of Hell, and I will personally welcome every single one of you to the Hazbin Hotel today.” A ripple of excitement moved through the crowd. Someone near the back actually clapped. Lucifer let the moment breathe, then smiled wider. “A welcome means exactly that. You may look around. You may ask about rooms, rates, and the redemption program. You may not submit petitions, request private audiences, demand royal rulings, or attempt to marry my daughter on the premises. Those are separate appointments and they are not happening here.”

A collective, disappointed murmur. One soul near the front crumpled a scroll with visible bitterness.

“However.” Lucifer’s gaze slid to Alastor, who was still leaning against the desk as if none of this concerned him. “The gentleman who so generously advertised this open house on the airwaves will be delighted to lead the tour and answer all of your questions about the hotel itself. Alastor?” He gestured with open, theatrical invitation. “The floor is yours.”

Every head turned.

Alastor’s smile did not change, but something behind the eyes sharpened for half a second. He straightened, cane tapping once against the floor, and stepped forward with the same unhurried grace he used when the broadcast light came on.

“Of course,” he said, voice warm and rolling. “What a pleasure. If you’ll all follow me, we’ll begin with the lobby and the remarkable history of this establishment…”

Lucifer stayed where he was long enough to watch Alastor absorb the first wave of actual hotel questions—rates, meal plans, whether the piano was functional—and only then stepped down. Charlie shot him a look that was equal parts gratitude and worry. He gave her a small shrug that tried to say *I’ve got this* and mostly said *I’m winging it*.

The tour moved.

Alastor led them through the rebuilt common rooms with the practiced cadence of someone who had already decided the narrative. Lucifer followed at the edge of the group, still carrying the two unused signs, answering the occasional hopeful glance with a firm shake of the head. A few visitors tried to peel off toward him anyway; Vaggie intercepted them with the efficiency of someone who had already reached her daily limit of nonsense.

They had barely reached the courtyard when a woman near the front raised her hand. She was older, sharp-eyed, wearing a coat that had seen better centuries.

“If someone checks in,” she said, “and decides halfway through that they’re done trying—what happens? Do you keep the room deposit? Is there a contract? What am I actually agreeing to if I stay?”

Lucifer felt the small, mean spark of anticipation. Alastor would pivot. He would make a joke at her expense, or at Charlie’s, or simply invent something charming and useless. That was the point of putting him on the spot.

Alastor turned to the woman without missing a beat.

“You agree to try,” he said. “That’s the only requirement. The rooms are paid week by week. If you decide the work is no longer for you, you settle the current week and leave. No further obligation, no black mark, no dramatic consequences beyond the ones you already carry. The hotel exists so that the attempt is possible. It does not exist to punish you for stopping.”

The wording was clean. Practical. Almost gentle.

Charlie’s face did something small and open, the way it did when someone repeated her own language back to her without realizing it. Lucifer watched the exchange and felt the satisfaction of the earlier maneuver cool into something tighter.

He had set Alastor up to stumble in public. Instead he had watched the Radio Demon deliver Charlie’s answer—her exact framing, her refusal to trap people—as if he had heard it enough times to wear the shape of it.

The tour continued. Alastor moved them toward the dining hall, still smiling, still fielding questions. Lucifer stayed at the back of the group, the handmade sign’s siblings under his arm, and let the new fact settle where it could not be immediately answered.

## Chapter 2: The Tour

Alastor ushered the group through the double doors into the dining hall, still mid-sentence about the virtues of communal meals and the hotel’s commitment to “structured optimism.” Lucifer followed at the rear, the two unused signs now leaning against a sideboard where he’d left them. The room still smelled of new varnish and the faint, hopeful scent of whatever Husk had been coerced into brewing earlier.

“—and of course the seating is arranged so that no one is forced into isolation unless they specifically request it, a detail Charlie insisted—”

“I was here first.”

The interruption cut across Alastor’s cadence like a dropped tray. Two demons near the middle of the group had squared off. One was the lanky soul with the petition scroll, now crumpled but still clutched. The other was a shorter, broader figure in a coat that looked expensive and slightly singed.

“You heard him,” the second one said. “No private audiences. That means we wait our turn like civilized—”

“I have a legitimate claim. A small duchy. The King can settle the paperwork in thirty seconds and we both know it.”

“And I have a business proposal that could fund this entire floor for a year. He’ll want to hear that before your sob story.”

Alastor’s smile stayed in place, but the tip of his cane tapped once against the floorboards. The rest of the group had stopped moving. A few looked embarrassed. Most looked interested in the outcome.

Charlie stepped forward before Lucifer could.

“Excuse me.” Her voice carried without rising. “Both of you. There’s no queue for the King today. There isn’t going to be one.” She looked from one to the other, then out at the wider circle of faces. “If you’re here because you want to see the hotel, you’re welcome to finish the tour. If you’re here because you want something from my father, that isn’t happening. Not in this building. Not today.”

The lanky one opened his mouth. Charlie didn’t wait.

“Vaggie. Can you take anyone who’s still hoping for an audience and walk them back to the front doors? Politely. And if anyone tries to leave a petition on the desk, throw it out.”

Vaggie’s wings flicked once in pure relief. “Gladly.”

Lucifer stayed quiet. He watched Charlie’s shoulders stay level, watched the way she didn’t glance at him for permission or backup. She had already decided. When two or three demons looked toward him anyway, he only raised both hands in a small, theatrical shrug that said *you heard the princess*.

The room emptied in stages. Some left with the brittle dignity of people who had wasted an afternoon. A few muttered about false advertising. Most simply filed out once it became clear there was nothing left to wait for. Alastor watched them go with the same pleasant expression he wore when a broadcast ended on schedule. Lucifer could not tell whether he was annoyed or merely recalculating.

When the last of the hopefuls had gone, the dining hall felt larger and strangely quiet. Only the woman from the courtyard remained, standing a little apart near one of the long tables, hands folded over the strap of a worn shoulder bag.

Charlie turned to her. “You’re still here.”

The woman nodded once. “I heard what he said in the courtyard. About trying. I don’t know if I can do a whole week of anything. But I could do one night. See if the place feels like somewhere a person could stay without already being sure they’re going to change.”

Charlie’s face did that open, careful thing again. “We can do one night. No deposit beyond the night itself. You come back with whatever you need, we give you a room, and in the morning you decide if you want to stay longer. No pressure either way.”

The woman considered that, then gave a short, dry laugh. “All right. I’ll come back with a bag. Don’t hold a parade.”

She left the way the others had, through the lobby doors, and the hotel settled into an afternoon quiet that felt almost unfamiliar after the press of the crowd.

Lucifer watched Charlie stand in the middle of the empty dining hall with the handmade sign still tucked under one arm. She looked at the doors the woman had used, then down at the sign, then up at nothing in particular. The hope on her face was smaller than it had been that morning, and more careful, but it was still there—directed now at the single person who had asked for a chance instead of a favor.

Lucifer said nothing. He simply stayed where he was and let her have the moment.

The open house had emptied hours ago. Lucifer was still in the lobby, turning one of the unused signs over in his hands for something to do with them, when the front doors opened again.

The woman from the courtyard stepped inside carrying a single battered suitcase and a paper bag that looked like it held toiletries and not much else. She set both down by the desk with the careful economy of someone who did not want to take up more space than necessary.

“I’m back,” she said. “One night. Do I need to fill anything out, or do I just… pick a key?”

Charlie had gone upstairs to check on the upper floors. Alastor was already behind the desk, as if he had been expecting the exact moment she would return. Lucifer stayed where he was, half-shadowed by the new staircase, and watched.

“Quiet, if you’ve got it,” the woman added. “I’m not looking to be anyone’s success story tonight. Just a room that doesn’t face the street.”

Alastor’s smile was mild. He opened a drawer, selected a key without hesitation, and set it on the desk between them. “Third floor, rear corridor. The windows look over the courtyard. You’ll hear the piano if someone plays, but not the traffic.”

She signed the guest register in a neat, compact hand. Alastor waited until she had finished, then left the book open to the fresh page and angled it slightly toward the stairs—exactly where Charlie would see the entry the moment she came down. Lucifer noticed the placement and felt the familiar, low irritation of not knowing which motive sat underneath it. Whether Alastor wanted Charlie to walk into proof that the day had produced a real guest, or whether he wanted the credit for having made the day produce one, or whether he simply enjoyed arranging the evidence so that both things happened at once.

The woman took the key, picked up her suitcase, and headed for the stairs without asking for an escort. Alastor watched her go with the same pleasant attention he gave a broadcast that was performing exactly as planned.

Only when her footsteps had faded did Lucifer push away from the banister.

“If you use my name on the air again,” he said, voice even, “you’d better be prepared to broadcast my reply as well.”

Alastor turned the smile on him without missing a beat. “Of course. Shall we schedule it? I have a mid-morning slot that tends to draw a reliable audience. Or would you prefer evening, when the after-work crowd is listening?”

Lucifer held his gaze for a moment longer than was polite. The grievance was still real. The lobby was still emptier than it should have been for the effort Charlie had put in. And Alastor was still standing there looking quietly satisfied with the turnout he had manufactured.

“I’ll let you know,” Lucifer said, and left it there.

---

The radio booth was still lit when Lucifer reached the end of the corridor. He had one of the unused signs under his arm—the gold-leaf monstrosity, too heavy for the front desk and too formal for anywhere else—and he used the edge of it to nudge the door open.

Alastor looked up from the console as if he had been expecting company at half past midnight. The smile arrived on schedule.

“Your Majesty. To what do I owe the pleasure?”

Lucifer held up the sign. “I need somewhere to put this that isn’t the lobby. Figured you’d know.”

Alastor pointed with the end of his cane, neat and immediate. “Storage cupboard. Directly across the hall. First door on the left. Impossible to miss.”

Lucifer glanced at the door in question. It was, in fact, impossible to miss. The easy exit sat right there, well-lit and waiting. He looked at it long enough to make the choice deliberate, then stepped fully into the booth and set the sign against the doorframe instead.

“How did you know her answer?” he said. “The one about leaving whenever you want. Word for word.”

Alastor’s smile didn’t change, but the angle of it did. “Charlie practices with me. She asks me to play the difficult guest—the one who wants the loophole, the penalty clause, the proof that the hotel is a trap with better wallpaper. I interrupt. She answers until the wording feels solid. Today’s question was one we’d already worn smooth.”

Lucifer pictured it without meaning to: Charlie in this same small room or the empty dining hall, running the same lines until they stopped sounding like hope and started sounding like policy. Alastor across from her, smiling, supplying the worst questions on purpose. Lucifer had spent the entire day bristling at how easily the Radio Demon spoke for her in public. The idea that she sometimes asked him to listen first sat in a different place entirely.

He let the silence stretch until it became useful.

“I can think of one you might have missed,” he said.

Alastor’s ears tipped forward a fraction. “Oh?”

“Someone checks in. They try. They make progress. Then they ask what happens if they succeed—really succeed—and Heaven still won’t take them. What does the hotel owe them then? A permanent room? An apology? A different kind of lie?”

Alastor’s fingers stilled on the edge of the console. For a moment the only sound was the low electrical hum of the equipment.

“That’s a sharper edge than most of them carry,” he said at last. “I’d be interested in your answer.”

Lucifer almost smiled. The irritation was still there—the broadcast, the petitions, the way Alastor had turned Charlie’s open house into his own stage—but so was the small, spiteful satisfaction of watching him have to wait for it.

“Maybe later,” Lucifer said. “When I’m sure you’re not going to put it on the air without asking.”

Alastor’s smile returned, slower this time. He shifted one of the chairs a few inches away from the console, angling it toward the open space between them. An invitation, or the closest thing he offered to one.

Lucifer didn’t sit. He also didn’t leave.

Behind him the gold-leaf sign stayed where he had left it, leaning against the doorframe.
