## Chapter 1

Jayce had always enjoyed Progress Day, ever since he was a boy. Seeing the various new inventions and technology for display and wonder in the galleries, the market stalls, the food! He loved the fireworks, the merry-making, everything.

His father had loved Progress Day too. Always proud to show off the latest Talis metalworking. Now he was gone, there was no one to man the Talis exhibition. Jayce had broken away from family tradition when he became a scientist, and his mother would never deprive him of his dream.

Viktor, Jayce noticed, liked Progress Day decidedly less than him. He didn't hate it exactly – as a scientist he too could see the benefit of the exhibitions and displays, and he enjoyed seeing the innovation – but Jayce suspected the Zaunite disliked the day on principle.

*Gaudy shows of wealth and privilege,* he'd called it last year, when the two of them had gone together to peruse the various exhibitions. *“Half of these creations have no practical use at* *all, it is science for the sake of status. Hextech won't be like that.”*

There were times that Jayce forgot Viktor was from Zaun. That wasn't an accident of course, Viktor made an effort, not to conceal his heritage, but to at least put it in the back of people's minds. It was only when he scoffed and muttered in his mother tongue some scathing insult or passive aggressive comment, or when he whacked Jayce entirely too readily and too accurately with his cane without even needing to look to aim, or when he shuffled uncomfortably to the nearest wall at a party Jayce dragged him to, while he glared at the politicians in their opulent clothing offering him thinly veiled patronising remarks on his work and *how proud they must be of you in Zaun* , that Jayce remembered the huge class divide between his world, and Viktor’s.

Jayce looked at Progress Day with awe and pride, Viktor looked at it with anger and resentment. For all of Piltover's ‘progress’, the undercity sank further into decline.

The first two years of their partnership Viktor had come along with him to the exhibitions, his face soft and indulgent as he followed him around, sharing in his appreciation for the latest technological advances. The third he declined the invitation, locking himself in the lab to work, frustrated that they had nothing to present themselves on the day to show for all their hard work.

He was always impatient. Science didn't just happen overnight, Jayce understood this, and he knew Viktor did too. But Viktor's need for advancement only grew by the year, his hunger for progress voracious in the wake of their slow pace. Jayce admired his drive – found it incredibly attractive even, without it they wouldn't have gotten close to where they were now – but it scared him sometimes, the wild glint he got in his eye when he went on rants at 3am or when he conducted a dangerous experiment with little to no safety equipment on (the latest attempt had singed half his eyebrow off, but Viktor had maintained that at least they were making progress).

“You sure you don't want to come with me this year? We've been invited to the Academy party this evening.” Jayce was saying as he adjusted his cravat in the reflection of a metal cog.

“Oh, an invitation to another party? Well, that changes everything.” Viktor drawled, not looking up from his notes. “In that case, I most *definitely* will *not* be going.”

Jayce sighed, looking over at his partner. “I thought it would be a good opportunity to look for investors, and get more funding. We've run up our budget again this month.”

“Yes, by all means go and beg.” Viktor muttered, lifting his pencil to poke at the runestones he was attempting to activate. “You'll be more successful playing the charming golden boy without me there, I'm sure. No one wants to give funding to a grumpy Zaunite.”

“You're not grumpy,” Jayce defended.

“I am on Progress Day.” Viktor replied shortly.

Jayce didn't have a reply to that. Viktor was testy at the best of times, but today his temper seemed to be on a shorter leash than usual. He stared at the back of his partner's head, the silence stretching on long enough that Viktor's conscience caught up to him, and he sighed.

“Forgive me Jayce, that was uncalled for. I am…preoccupied.” He murmured.

“By the prototype you've been working on? You'll crack it V, you're a genius.” Jayce came to stand behind his surly companion, resting a hand on his shoulder. “What's the prognosis?”

Viktor sighed, sitting back in his chair. He reached up, covering Jayce's hand with his own. Jayce's heart fluttered erratically.

Viktor hummed, tapping his pencil against his lips in a way that really shouldn't be as attractive as it was.

“It is not the power that is the problem – it's how it's conducted. Once active, the runes spin out of control. I've increased the velocity of the gyration, and it helps a little, but ultimately the power produced proves too much for the apparatus to contain. I've been using mirrors to redirect the beams to minimize damage, but even that is unpredictable.”

Jayce looked up at the burn marks on the walls, the hole that had been burned clean through a metal beam in the ceiling.

Viktor rubbed a tired hand over his face. “We've burned through our allotted budget for materials, and I don't even know if I can get this prototype *working* .”

“What's the longest you've had it running so far? Before it melts down?” Jayce asked.

“34 minutes. No metal I've worked with is strong enough to withstand the heat produced. It melts right through it.”

“Hm. I could go home, see what I can get at the forge.”

“No, Jayce.” Viktor shook his head. “You don't need to beg from your family to fix my miscalculations.”

“It's not begging, mom won't have a problem with me taking some off scraps from the forge.” Jayce said. He chuckled dryly. “Honestly it's better than whoring myself out at these parties for funding.”

Viktor stilled under his hand. He turned his head to look up at him. His eyes flashed with something steely, and his voice took on a hard edge.

“What do you mean *whoring* yourself out?”

“Oh, well I mean – not exactly like *that* it's just – you know I've got to get all primped and preened like this and have to talk to the right people and say the right things and endure their damn touches and loaded compliments. Aristocrats flirt so much Viktor, you've no idea –”

“Has anyone taken you to bed?” Viktor cut in abruptly.

Jayce spluttered to a halt. “W- *what* ?”

“Have you had to sleep with any of our sponsors in order to keep funding my failed experiments?” Viktor sounded insistent, his gaze as hard as granite, fixing Jayce in place.

“No! No, no I haven't…I've been *asked,* or propositioned more like but I've never – no one's *forced* me Viktor. I'm not that kind of guy, I don't…I don't just sleep around, especially not for that reason.”

Viktor finally let go of his gaze with a sigh, relaxing into his chair again. “I believe you, Jayce. I'm sorry I got so – well. I'm sorry. I don't mean to pry into your personal affairs.”

“You were worried.” Jayce realised.

“Of course I was worried.” Viktor said, like he was offended that it wasn't already obvious to Jayce. “I am the reason we are at the end of our budget already this month, it's bad enough you even need to parade around at these functions to get our money, but if you were being coerced into trading *sexual* *favors* for funding because of *me* –”

“V, don't worry.” Jayce squeezed his shoulder. “I'm a big boy, I can take care of myself, don't worry, I'm not going to let anyone sweet talk me into bed with the promise of money.”

“Mm, you're also a *štěňátko* , Jayce.” Viktor said, the drawled vowels rolling over his tongue in a way that made Jayce's skin tingle.

“What does that mean?” He asked.

Viktor hummed, looking for a translation in his brain.

“It means puppy.” He said, and Jayce felt his face begin to burn. “More specifically it means *little* puppy. Used to refer to someone who is sweet but naive. Which is you.”

He glanced up at Jayce. If he noticed his burning cheeks, he didn't mention it.

“You are kind Jayce, almost too kind.” Viktor’s voice took on a softer note.

The kind of tone one might take with a lover. *Not a lover, a loved one. A friend. I'm* *his* *friend*. Jayce reminded himself.

“You're the kind of person others will seek to take advantage of. You see good in people, a rare trait which I admire about as much as I abhor it, but that good heart makes you an easy target to manipulate. You wouldn't last a second in the undercity, you'd likely give all your money to the first street urchin looking to scam you, and walk out of there without so much as the clothes on your back. We have a name for people like you in my hometown.”

“Which is?”

“ *Blbec na entou.* It means a fool to the ninth degree. You'd be eaten alive down there. And yes, I worry the same applies here. The parties here may have more gold and champagne, but it's the same manipulation and the same power grabbing as in Zaun.”

“I'm not *that* stupid.” Jayce defended, his nose wrinkling. “I do know when I'm being manipulated, V. At least here in Piltover anyway.”

“Uh-huh.”

“In any case, I appreciate you worrying about me. It's nice to know you care.” He clasped his hand over Viktor's other shoulder. “And the Zaunite language sounds really nice in your voice.”

“Of course that's what you took from that.” Viktor muttered, though there was a heat to his cheeks that wasn't there before.

“Anyway, I'd better get going. You sure I can't convince you to even come with me to the exhibitions today?” Jayce straightened up and once again checked his appearance in the nearest reflective surface.

“No, no I will…I think I will be going home today.” Viktor said.

“Okay, well I'll stop by your apartment on the way to the party?”

“No, I meant I'll be going to the undercity.” Viktor’s clarification had Jayce's hands stilling on his cravat.

“The undercity? Why, what's going on there today?” He asked, a slight furrow in his brow.

Viktor had the insane urge to kiss that furrow away.

“There's just something important I have to do.” Viktor told him. “I'll come back to work at the lab when I'm done. I'll be back before you go to your party tonight to check your stupid tie and make sure your hair is still presentable.”

Viktor went back to his notes, but Jayce stayed a moment, watching him with that same frown.

He didn't know how he felt about Viktor going back to the undercity. He didn't want to express his concern, but he was worried about Viktor going down there. The undercity was dangerous, and with Viktor’s condition – he couldn't ask him to stay topside for his safety, that would just piss Viktor off and make him more determined to go, he hated it when Jayce worried about him and Jayce knew perfectly well that Viktor grew up in Zaun and was perfectly capable of handling himself – but the thought of Viktor limping his way down in the dark, toxic infested streets filled him with a terror he couldn't quite define.

“Jayce,” Viktor turned his head slightly, catching his gaze out the corner of his eye. There was a hint of a smile on his lips. “I will be careful.”

Jayce let out a breath he didn't know he'd been holding. “Thank you. How do you do that?”

“Do what?”

“Know everything I'm thinking.”

Viktor chuckled, the soft sound messing with Jayce's heart almost as much as that little smirk on Viktor’s lips did.

“You're *moje štěňátko* , Jayce. You're not hard to read.” He said warmly.

Jayce recognised the ‘little puppy’ part, though the additional word was unfamiliar to him. He frowned quizzically at Viktor, who just smirked at him and refused to translate.

“You should get going Jayce, you'll miss the opening ceremony. You love the opening ceremony.” Viktor said.

He did love the opening ceremony. He loved that Viktor knew he loved the opening ceremony. He'd love to take Viktor to the opening ceremony. He loved Viktor.

“Right, right.” Jayce said distractedly. “I'll meet you back here this afternoon?”

Viktor nodded. “Have fun, Jayce. See you soon.”

Jayce adjusted the lapels of his coat as he strode through the bustling streets, his spirits lifted by the vibrant energy of Progress Day. The city was alive with celebration: children darted between market stalls, their faces alight with wonder as merchants demonstrated miniature automaton toys and brightly colored gadgets. Street performers juggled glowing spheres to the delight of onlookers, while the scent of roasted nuts and spiced pastries filled the crisp air.

Jayce chuckled as he stepped around a man attempting to corral an unruly clockwork dog, the automaton barking with startling realism. He stopped briefly to admire a display of levitating baubles, marveling at the delicate lattice work holding the crystals in place. The air above him burst into light as the first of the fireworks streaked across the sky, drawing gasps of awe from the crowd. For a moment, the world felt perfect—brimming with the promise of progress and invention.

Elsewhere, Viktor was steeling himself as he got ready to make the trip below. He tied his cravat in the same way he'd practised years before, when he'd first wandered into the Academy halls wearing a homemade uniform and acting like he belonged there. It was the only knot he'd ever learned, he hadn't felt the need to learn any other ways, but at the last Academy function he'd attended, one of the more distinguished guests commented on it's old fashioned-ness. Viktor had bristled. Jayce had complimented him. All in all, it had been a good night just to hear Jayce's words.

Viktor sighed, what had he become? Preening in the mirror like a Piltie. Chasing the compliments and the smile of one particular Piltie.

But Jayce wasn't like others. He meant the things he said, and he liked Viktor. He didn't look at him like he was dirt beneath his shoe, or a charity case. And in any case, it wasn't any crime to fall in love with –

Viktor realised the tie he had put on was Jayce's. He had gifted it to him not long after they had begun working together, for the new year he'd said. It was red, with delicate gold trim. The Talis insignia nestled at his throat.

He *couldn* 't wear this to the undercity. He ripped it off him as if the fabric had offended him, staring at the insignia.

*“So you're branding me.”*

*“What? What do you mean, Vik?”*

*“The Talis insignia on this so-called gift of yours. What, you have to slap your mark of* *ownership on me like I'm some sort of pet?”*

*“What?! No! No, V that wasn't – I'm sorry, that was an accident I –”*

*“Ah, forgive me. You* accidentally *gifted me something to wear that bears your family crest* *right where everyone can see it. Give the Zaunite some semblance of worth by attaching him* *to a House name.”*

*“No! No V that isn't what – I'm sorry, I'm really sorry!”*

*“If that wasn't your intention, then what was?”*

*“It was a mistake, honestly. I forgot to mention to the tailor that it wasn't for me. And then I* *saw it and I just thought…the colours would suit you.”*

*“You…you thought the colours would…suit me?”*

*“Red looks nice on you, V. That's all I was thinking, honestly.”*

*“Well…if that's all there is to the gift then, I'll accept it. I…thank you, Jayce. It is…a* *beautiful tie.”*

Viktor began sliding the tie around his neck again. Wearing it to Zaun may be inadvisable, but something in him wanted them to see him in it. Wearing the insignia of his partner's House, something dignified, something honourable. Something that said he'd *made* something of himself while he was away.

He wasn't just some poor Academy student anymore, he wasn't just the assistant to the Dean anymore, he was a renowned scientist, co-founder and co-inventor of Hextech. Partner to Jayce Talis, Piltover's Golden Boy. He wasn't just Viktor of Zaun with nothing to his name.

Part of him didn't like attaching his own worth to his relationship with Jayce. It just proved the snobs of the upper class correct, that a Zaunite couldn't be worth anything unless a Piltie was behind them.

But as he smoothed down the cravat and looked in the mirror, his fingers dragging over the Talis insignia, he felt warmth bloom in his chest.

He wasn't Jayce's *property* , he was a valued partner and friend. And that was something he was proud of.

He checked his clothing again. He'd tried to dress simply – his wardrobe was fairly simple in any case, and hardly extensive, but having even two sets of clothes was a privilege he'd not experienced until he left Zaun – but the fabrics were richer than anything available in the undercity.

He hoped they weren't noticeable enough to cause him trouble, but enough to show his success. Well, success felt like a generous word. He didn't feel like he'd achieved much – Hextech was getting off the ground, yes, but it was nowhere near ready to produce the kind of technology he and Jayce dreamed of. It would be a few years away, still. Time was ticking.

The sun was shining when he left. He stepped carefully around happy citizens in the streets, patting his vest pocket to ensure the keys to the lab were still with him, and pulling on his coat.

Viktor made his way towards the bridge to the undercity, his steps slower and more deliberate than usual. His cane struck the cobblestones with rhythmic precision, each tap a reminder of the uneven terrain ahead. Idly, he watched the birds fly by.

Birds fascinated him. He'd never seen one as a child, but he'd sometimes heard them through the pipes in the morning. His first day at the Academy, he'd sat in his dorm room and stared out the window, transfixed by the bird making a nest in the tree nearby.

Fascinated by its freedom to fly. An idea that stuck in his head ever since. What that feeling of weightlessness would be like, the thrill of lifting off from the confines of the ground and floating higher than anyone ever had.

An experience he'd finally got on the night he met Jayce. It was one of the fondest memories he had, and the memory of them floating in the air in Heimerdinger's office amidst the blue glow brought a wistful smile to his face.

It had been the start of Viktor’s life, in a sense. The start of everything. His life with Jayce was…everything. He wasn't sure if Jayce felt the same, but that night he was certain he would do anything for that bright eyed intelligent man and his dream. Their dream.

The air grew colder as he descended the sloping streets, and with every step, the noise of the celebration above felt more distant, more detached from the usual joy of Progress Day.

Perhaps the cooling air was purely a psychosomatic response, but he always felt colder the closer he got to Zaun.

He hadn’t been back to Zaun in years. Not since before… well, before *everything* . He told himself he was returning for a good reason—to pay his respects to his parents, to stand before the memorial plaques he hadn’t visited since their deaths—but the excuses rang hollow in his ears.

Why was he even going? Of all days, why today? He could have easily accepted Jayce's invitation to attend today's celebrations. He could have even grumbled at tonight's party and amused himself with watching Jayce spin his pretty smiles and convincing words to suck money from the elite.

As much as he loathed crowds and parties, he would have preferred Jayce's company today.

Viktor paused at the edge of the lift platform, gripping his cane tightly. He looked out over the shadowed expanse below, the haze of green and grey smog coiling above the rooftops like an oppressive shroud. For all the time he’d spent in Piltover, Zaun had never truly left him. It lived in the cracks of his hands, in the sharp lilt of his accent when he was too tired to suppress it, in the memories that haunted him when he closed his eyes.

He felt the familiar pang of guilt gnawing at his ribs. He’d left Zaun behind. He’d justified it as a necessity for his work, for his dreams, but deep down, he knew he’d abandoned more than just a city. He’d abandoned the people, the grime, the struggle—all in exchange for polished marble floors and finely tailored suits.

What would his parents think if they could see him now? Would they be proud of the man he’d become? Or ashamed of the boy who’d turned his back on the life they’d built for him? They had wanted him to come topside, they'd sacrificed everything for him to get there, to get away from the misery and the suffering.

But in the end, he'd left them behind. And they'd died in the dark, surrounded by the filth and desperation.

He showed his bridge pass and his identification papers to the Enforcer by the lift gates. She looked his papers over for a moment before waving him on.

The lift creaked into motion, and Viktor felt his stomach twist. He wondered briefly how the people of his old neighborhood would see him now, if they even remembered him at all. He imagined the whispers: the prodigal son, back at last, dressed in Piltover's finery. Would they sneer at him, curse his name? Or worse—would they see through him entirely, recognize the frail and conflicted man beneath the polished exterior?

The lift hummed as it descended, the sound almost drowned out by the buzzing discomfort in Viktor’s chest. The air, thick with smog, seemed to press against his lungs, and for the first time since he’d left, Viktor felt the weight of Zaun's air—stale and acrid, a far cry from the crisp cleanliness of Piltover.

He clenched his fists, fingers trembling around his cane as the lift groaned to a halt. The familiar metallic scent of oil and rust filled his nostrils, and for a moment, Viktor found himself unable to move. The doors slid open, and the noise of the streets rushed in, loud and jarring. A cacophony of shouting vendors, clanking machinery, and the distant hum of the city’s underbelly.

As Viktor stepped out, the reality of Zaun hit him in waves. The streets were narrower than he remembered, suffocatingly packed with makeshift stalls selling scraps of metal and spoiled food. Shadows clung to every corner, not just from the perpetual gloom, but from the hunched figures of children scavenging through the trash. He passed a woman coughing violently, her shoulders shaking under a threadbare shawl. No one stopped to help her. A man leaned against a wall, his face gaunt, staring blankly ahead as if waiting for the city to swallow him whole.

The rancid smell of rot and chemicals burned Viktor’s nostrils, and his gait faltered. His cane slipped on a slick patch of oil, and he stumbled, catching himself against a rusted pipe. Once, he’d navigated these streets without a second thought. Now, the uneven ground, the narrow alleys, the suffocating crowds—they overwhelmed him. He hated how foreign it felt, how detached he’d become from the place that shaped him.

He passed a dilapidated factory where skeletal workers hauled loads far heavier than their frames could bear. Their faces, streaked with grime, barely looked up as he moved past. A child—barely old enough to walk—sat near the factory gate, his small hands clutching a broken toy. The boy’s eyes were hollow, unblinking, and Viktor’s chest tightened at the sight.

It wasn’t just that Zaun had always been cruel; it was worse now. The rot had deepened, the despair thickened. Viktor had left with hopes of rising above this—of finding answers in science to mend what was broken. But what had really changed? For all his efforts, Zaun remained trapped in its cycle of suffering, and Viktor felt the sting of his failure like a blade in his side.

The path to his old neighborhood grew darker, the oppressive glow of green chem-lights illuminating the grime that clung to every surface. The walls seemed to sag under the weight of rust and mildew, while pipes hissed with leaks that dripped unknown fluids onto the cobblestones. His cane tapped against the ground, the sound swallowed by the hum of machinery and the distant cries of a heated argument. He forced himself to keep moving, but with every step, the sorrow in his chest grew heavier.

Zaun had been his home, once. But as Viktor looked around, he wondered if it ever truly could be again.

Vykodost was not the neighborhood it once was. Viktor’s gaze swept over the streets as he stepped out onto the concrete, feet scraping against the rough, uneven pavement. He remembered when the lights had shone a little brighter here, when the laughter of children had echoed across the alleyways, when his mother’s voice had carried across their small home. But now, the glow of lanterns was dim and erratic, flickering like the last vestiges of hope in the faces of the residents. Vykodost had always been a place of immigrants— Zaunites, Piltovans, refugees from the Eastern Isles, and those just trying to make a life. Once, the different accents had mixed like a melody, creating a hum of unity and survival. Now it felt like the discordant notes of a broken song.

Viktor’s boots crunched over the gravel, and he paused to take in the sight of the alleyway that led to his family’s old home. Once, the walls had been bright with murals—images of prosperity, of hope. His father had painted them, along with the others who had lived in Vykodost. They were a symbol of resilience, a testament to the belief that, no matter where you came from, you could build a life here.

Now, the murals were peeling, the vibrant colors faded to pale, washed-out pastels. A few of them had been replaced by crude, hastily scrawled graffiti—threats, curses, the anger of those who had been left behind in Piltover’s shadow. Viktor’s breath caught in his throat. Had he really forgotten how bad it had gotten?

He wanted to turn away. The streets felt too narrow, the walls too close, the air too heavy with memories he had buried long ago. But he couldn’t. He owed it to this place, to the people who had raised him, to see it with his own eyes. To see what had become of the dreams his parents had held, the ones that had led them to Vykodost in the first place.

He walked deeper into the heart of the district, the weight of the cane on his arm, the strain in his shoulders tightening with every step. The children that used to run alongside the streets now hung in doorways, their faces hard and wary. They looked like their parents, the same stubborn glint in their eyes, but there was a cruelty in their gazes that hadn’t been there before. Were they born to survive or born to fight?

He passed the bakery where his mother had worked, the smell of stale bread mixing with the oily stench of the city. The sign above the door, once bright and welcoming, now hung crooked, and the glass windows were cracked.

Viktor felt a pang in his chest as he watched an old woman, hunched over a cart, trying to push it across the uneven ground. Her face was drawn, lined with the exhaustion of years of struggle. She didn’t notice him at first, her hands moving steadily as she sorted through the scraps of fabric piled high in her cart. It was only when he stopped in front of her, his cane tapping lightly on the cobblestones, that she looked up.

For a moment, she didn’t recognize him. Her eyes were clouded, fogged with age and hardship, and her lips parted as if to say something—before the recognition flickered in her gaze.

“Viktor…?” Her voice was cracked, as if it had been years since it had been used in any meaningful way.

He swallowed hard, his throat closing up. “Hello, Miss Pavela.”

She stared at him, and for a long time, neither of them said anything. It was as if the years between them had only made the distance greater. The life Viktor had lived in Piltover seemed to have built a wall between him and the world he had once belonged to.

“You’ve changed,” she finally muttered, her gaze lingering on his clothes, the fine fabric, the polished shoes, the gold stitching of his cuffs. “You look like you've done well for yourself, you looked like a Piltie for a moment there. Didn't recognise you for a moment, *maluszek* .”

Viktor flinched, the words striking harder than they should have. He opened his mouth, but nothing came out.

In a startling moment, he wondered if he'd forgotten how to speak Zaunite. The old syllables washed over his ears as she spoke, and it took a moment to jumpstart his brain to understand it.

He wanted to explain, to tell her that he hadn’t forgotten, that he had tried—tried so hard to escape the fate that had bound them all to the grime and corruption of this place. But it wasn’t the time for explanations. He had no right.

“I…” His voice faltered, the sounds of his language sounding harsh on his tongue. “I suppose I have done well.”

The old woman smiled, and it reminded him of his mother so painfully he nearly broke down sobbing. She held out her arms and pulled him into a strong hug. He fumbled, awkwardly bending his arms to pat her back.

“You must come inside and tell me all about it.” She insisted, ushering him into the bakery. “I've just taken a batch of miniature *makowce* if you'd like one.”

“You still make them?” Viktor felt himself smiling. At least some things stayed the same.

“Of course, best sellers.”
