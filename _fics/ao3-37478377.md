**You think I’m unkind**

By the time Katara knocks, he’s been waiting around half the night. His car is in the shop, and he shouldn’t be so resentful that his sister is too busy working miracles to take him out to the Mexican place downtown, the one with the custom guac, but just because it’s a first-world problem doesn’t mean he can’t be pissed by the time she shows up almost two hours late.

He’s planning to say something acidic about punctuality, but when he opens the door Katara looks like she’s seen a ghost.

“Oh my God,” she says, dropping her purse on the floor.

“What?”

“It's *him*,” she hisses. “Down the hall. That's the guy I've been worried about.”

Sokka has to be honest: he has no idea who Katara is talking about. She calls him to unload about her days at the clinic and he mostly lets it wash over him, making the appropriate noises from time to time. It isn’t because he’s callous; it’s the opposite, he tells himself. If he takes it all in, he’ll just break his own heart too.

“The guy?”

“In 312. God, I can't believe I didn't see it before.”

“You're never over,” Sokka points out helpfully.

She wheels on him.

“Because you never entertain!”

“Because I have a shitty apartment!” he yells.

"Yeah, and whose fault is that?” she yells.

“It's not my fault it's fundamentally shitty!” Sokka pulls up short. “Wait, why are we even arguing about this? If I lived in a different apartment he wouldn't even be my neighbor.”

Katara looks like she's about to cry.

"Do you know how many times he's come in looking half-dead?” she asks limply. “Busted lip, broken fingers, mouth full of blood, contusions all down his jaw…" She reaches up absently to touch her face.

“When they moved in,” Sokka says slowly, “he already had the... you know.”

He’s only caught a flash of the guy: white face, red burn.

“I didn’t know how to find him,” she says. “I wanted to report it, but he didn’t want that. I get it, but it’s just… hard.” She sighs. "He goes by Li when he comes in.”

“I don’t know his name,” Sokka admits. "I’m pretty sure the name on the mailbox is, um. The other guy.”

He’s barely seen Li, but he sees Zhao all the time. Bulky, roid-ragey, with unflattering sideburns. It’s easy to project onto Zhao now, in hindsight, all sorts of unflattering qualities. Before Katara came, Sokka probably just thought he was a middle-management gym-bro.

Katara grabs his hands.

“Sokka,” she says urgently. “You should borrow Appa.”

He looks at her.

“I should do what now?”

“If you have Appa, you'll have an excuse to be walking by his place a couple times a day.”

“Why would I—”

“And maybe you'll talk, or he'll at least feel like someone is there to notice.”

Sokka rakes his hands through his hair.

“You know I'd do anything for you, Katara—”

“Good,” she says.

“But I'm not stepping into this.”

“I'm not asking you to break up fights,” Katara says. “I'm asking you to walk Appa a few times a day.”

“I know exactly what you're asking.”

Her eyes burn.

“You never think of anyone except yourself!”

“Katara—”

She grabs her purse off the floor.

“What about dinner?” he asks.

“You can have dinner alone," Katara says, opening the door. “With all the people you really care about.”

—

His first impression is that Li looks faded. He’s collecting the mail from the big, subdivided mailbox downstairs, all rows and rows of tiny slots, and he’s bundled up in a thick cardigan. His hair is long and messy, pulled back in a low ponytail, and his face is puffy, a little softness under the chin.

“Hey neighbor,” Sokka says awkwardly.

The man jumps, clutching his letters to his chest.

“Sorry, didn’t mean to… I just wanted to say—um. Well. I wanted to say what I just said, I guess?” Sokka rubs the back of his neck and reminds himself to look like this is going well. “I’m Sokka. I live a couple doors down from you. 317.”

He sticks out his hand and Li looks at it.

“Sorry,” Sokka says again, for some reason.

Li touches his hand tentatively, really barely making contact at all, and they shake. His hand vanishes back into the oversized cardigan.

“Zuko,” Li says.

“Sorry?”

Li clears his throat.

“My name is Zuko.”

His voice is hoarse, not early-morning hoarse. Almost damaged-hoarse.

“Oh!” Sokka says. “Well, it’s nice to meet you, Zuko. I hope, um. If you ever need anything—like normal neighbor stuff, you know, not anything special, not that I couldn’t also do something special—“ God, is it possible for Sokka to, just, evaporate? Or maybe sublimate? Go straight from solid into vapor and float off harmlessly into the sky?—“I mean, whatever,” he finishes lamely.

Zuko looks almost amused. His lips are quirked at one side. When he makes eye contact for a split second, his eyes are burning gold. It’s like a flash of someone else inside him, and then it’s gone.

“Good to know,” he says. And his eyes drop again, he locks up his mailbox, and he heads back upstairs.

**People aren’t the way you think they are**

Appa is a huge, excitable but gentle sheepdog who Sokka privately refers to as the Living Mop, or Moppy for short. Sometimes Moppa. He used to refer to him as the Mop non-privately, but Katara flicked him upside the head for it. Something about hurting Aang’s feelings.

“We doing okay, buddy?” Sokka asks, setting down a bowl of kibble. Appa goes at it earnestly. He’s a very earnest dog.

He's perfect for Aang, really, Sokka thinks, remembering that he promised he'd call every night after Aang finishes with his last client, so he can FaceTime with Appa. Then Aang will go back to charting. He got his MSW in the spring, and though he wouldn’t admit to being anything less than chuffed and perky, Sokka can tell he’s been overwhelmed with the amount of paperwork he has to do when he’d rather just be helping people.

“Would you like to go for a w-a-l-k after?”

Appa’s tail flops.

He tries taking Appa out at different times, hoping he might figure out when Zuko goes to collect the mail. He's conscious, too, that if he does this badly, Zuko will feel stalked, doubly intimidated, if Katara's right about what it's like inside 312.

A few days with no luck. Finally he bumps into Zuko near dawn, when the grass is crisp with frost under his slippers and Appa is snuffling along the pavement. True to form, Appa goes trotting up to Zuko like an old friend.

“Whoa there, buddy!” Sokka calls.

There's a snatch of fear in Zuko's face, seeing the dog. He backs up, dropping half the mail.

“Appa! Enough of that! Yip yip!”

Appa's ears perk up and he circles back to Sokka's feet, whining. Meanwhile Sokka bends down to pick up the mail, which has wound up mostly in a patch of icy mud.

"I'm sorry about that,” he says, offering the dirty envelopes to Zuko. “There goes your L.L. Bean catalogue.”

Zuko snorts before he catches himself.

“It's okay,” he says in his soft rasp. “I wasn't worried about the catalogue.”

“You don't seem like an L.L. Bean person,” Sokka says.

“What do you mean?”

Zuko is squatting down now, offering his fingers to Appa hesitantly. He's wearing the same big cardigan today, fuzzy where the weave has broken apart, and his hair is falling out of a sloppy bun. It strikes Sokka as odd, the way Zuko dresses. He seems like such a careful person, careful in every aspect of his personality, but you wouldn't know it from his presentation.

Not that he really knows Zuko at all.

When he says Zuko doesn't seem like an L.L. Bean person, he means that Zuko doesn't seem like someone who owns anything new or made to last. But he doesn't say that.

"They're very, um. Into duck boots and firewood tote bags.” Sokka clears his throat. “You can come closer. He's a good boy.”

He scratches behind Appa's ears and the dog goes brain-dead, conked out on bliss. His pink tongue sprawls out of his mouth.

“He's adorable,” Zuko says. “What's his name? Papa?”

“Moppa,” Sokka says without thinking. “Fuck. Appa! I just call him that because he looks like an animate mop.”

Zuko covers his mouth for some reason.

“He does,” he says. Appa is cleaning his fingers now with enthusiasm.

“Don't tell my sister that,” Sokka says. “She'll kill me.”

For just a moment, Zuko goes very still. It comes and goes in the space of a second, and then it's gone.

“So he's your sister's dog?” he asks casually.

“Yeah, my sister and her boyfriend's. He's just staying with me for a while.”

He hesitates. “Is everything okay with—?”

“Oh! Sorry, yeah. She's fine. They're just, um. Remodeling their kitchen,” Sokka says, because that seems like something people do. “You're so sweet to ask.”

Zuko covers his mouth again.

“Well, I hope I'll see him again,” he says. “He's, um. A good boy.”

“He is,” Sokka agrees. “And you will.”

He trots upstairs, and then it's just Sokka and the mop on the grass, the sun pinking the concrete sides of the apartment complex. It occurs to Sokka that Zuko covers his mouth when he's smiling.

It’s dawn that works. Zuko checks the mail at dawn and at 4 pm, usually just after the afternoon mail comes. Sokka wonders why he seems so anxious about the mail. Maybe there’s nothing else he can do? Maybe he’s bored? What if Zhao keeps him inside all day?

They talk lightly at the mailboxes, Sokka making nervous jokes about how Appa has the smallest bladder in the world and keeps waking him up at ungodly hours. Again, if Aang were here, it would break his heart, these imaginary aspersions cast on his dog.

Zuko always bends down and lets Appa sniff his fingers before reaching out to stroke his head. It’s a thoughtful thing to do, a good-with-animals thing to do, not wanting to scare or force yourself on some innocent thing that has no choice.

“He likes you,” Sokka says, as the sheepdog happily snorfles at Zuko’s hand.

Zuko looks up at him, eyes bright for a moment, before he drops them. “Are you sure?”

“Yeah, of course.” Sokka runs his hand down Appa’s back. “He loves you.”

He can only see one of Zuko’s ears, poking through his hair, but the shell has gone candy-pink.

“I’ve always thought being good with animals is a test of character,” Sokka says.

“You think so?"

“Animals don’t put up with assholes.”

Zuko hums like he’s considering something, stroking Appa’s ears. “We never had dogs growing up,” he says finally. “Or any animals. My father didn’t like them.”

“Or they didn’t like him,” Sokka says bluntly, before he can catch himself. Zuko looks up at him, stills for a moment, and then nods.

“I don’t think that’s wrong,” he says.

“But you’ve spent time with animals.”

“Yes.” Zuko doesn’t elaborate. “I’d like to have a cat,” he says quietly. The way he says it is the way little kids talk about being astronauts some day, like it’s a fantasy, an impossible dream you indulge while you’re waiting for the bus to arrive, for school to let out, for fourth period to flick into fifth.

He says it like it’s obvious it could never happen.

“Allergies?" Sokka asks lightly.

Zuko is shaking his head. He pats Appa in the way you pat animals when you’re about to wrap up, and then he gets to his feet.

“I just worry,” he says. “I worry about things happening to them.”

“You shouldn’t,” Sokka says. "You’d be a good cat dad.”

Zuko’s eyes turn hard.

“You don’t know me,” he says.

**Don’t you get the picture yet?**

When Zuko doesn’t show up the next morning, Sokka assumes it’s something he said. Granted, he has no idea what he’s done wrong—he called Zuko a potential cat dad? and Zuko, apparently, thought he wasn’t?—but Sokka has managed to get in more serious trouble over things he’d said, things he’d thought meant nothing at all, and this sort of situation is not new to him. He would give Zuko space, and then he’d apologize. If he doesn’t understand what he’s apologizing for... Well, it’s the thought that counts.

In the afternoon, still no Zuko. That evening, while Appa is FaceTiming and Aang is telling him what a good boy he is, Sokka lets his thoughts drift to implausible ways he can check on Zuko. Could he walk by casually and listen for sounds at the door? Could he knock and say he’d noticed no one had come to get their mail? Wasn’t that weird, to act like he was monitoring their mail? Even if he was?

The next morning, Appa is snuffling in the grass and then there’s Zuko, bundled up in the ugly cardigan, heading downstairs. Sokka has a feeling he doesn’t know how to explain; it’s barely a feeling at all, just a peculiar intensity of attention, like Zuko walking downstairs is the most important thing in the world right now. Appa bounds over and sniffs at Zuko’s slippers, whining.

“Hey!” Sokka says. “I was worried about you.”

Zuko smiles thinly. He looks a little off, somehow.

“You okay, buddy?”

“I get migraines sometimes,” Zuko says slowly, like putting words together is very hard.

Sokka pauses for a moment to consider whether he should let Zuko lie to him. He decides not to call him on it.

“I thought you were mad at me,” he says, finally.

Zuko covers his mouth, hiding his smile.

“What would I be mad at you about?”

“I dunno,” Sokka says, feeling like an idiot. “You seemed upset after I said you’d be a good cat dad.”

“Oh.” Zuko nervously digs his keys out of his pocket and starts fumbling at the mailboxes.

And he *is* fumbling. It’s like his fingers aren’t working properly, and he can’t get the key into the slot. It’s the most mundane thing, and Sokka can’t remember the last time he felt so scared.

“Zuko.”

“Mmm?"

“Let me help,” Sokka says, because he can’t say *what’s wrong with you?*

He takes the keys from Zuko’s hands, gently—his fingers are long and very cool, like he’s been out in the cold without gloves, and they open without resistance—and unlocks the mailbox for him.

“Mail,” he says uselessly, gesturing at the open door.

“I can see that,” Zuko says. He isn’t moving.

Something’s wrong, something is *very* wrong, and it’s making Sokka crazy, not being able to pinpoint what it is. It makes him feel like he has to cut the right wires before a bomb goes off, except the bomb is Zuko going back upstairs again.

Zuko pushes his hair behind his ears, nervously, and Sokka follows the gesture until he’s looking at Zuko’s face. Zuko, who is always un-fussed-with and unkempt, is wearing makeup. It’s pasty, slightly darker than the tone of his skin, and it trails across his right temple like the tail of a falling star.

“Your head,” Sokka says,

Zuko tilts his face, looking at him.

“My…?”

“You hurt your head,” Sokka says. Then he rearranges it: “Your head got hurt.”

His eyes are blazing. It’s panic, Sokka thinks. Now that they’re this close, he can see that one eye looks off, veering slightly in the wrong direction, the pupil blown.

Sokka takes his hands without thinking. Zuko doesn’t resist, maybe less because he doesn’t mind, more like he can’t figure out how. Sokka’s hands are much bigger, stronger, eclipsing his.

“Let me get you to the clinic,” he says. He can feel Zuko’s fingers trembling. “It’s, what, four thirty now? We can be there and back by six am.”

Zuko is shaking his head no. “Doesn’t work,” he says sadly.

“You should see a doctor.”

He’s still shaking his head.

“The thing is,” Sokka says, “actually. You *need* to see one.”

Zuko doesn’t look happy, but there’s no fight left in him. He squints at Sokka and sighs.

“Nine,” Zuko says. “Nine? I have to—it needs to be quiet.”

“I can do nine.”

Sokka realizes he’s still holding Zuko’s hands. He drops them.

“Can you help with the—” Zuko says, waving broadly at the mailboxes. “Can you get it out of the…?”

“Oh. Yeah. Sorry.”

Sokka hands him his mail, closes the door, wiggles the key out of the slot.

“Can you give me your number? Should I text you?”

“No,” Zuko says quickly. “I don’t have a phone."

“You don’t have a phone,” Sokka repeats. “Okay. So we’ll meet down here? “

“Okay,” he says. And then, like he’s talking to himself: “I can do that.”

—

It’s the first time he’s seen Zuko dressed like he’s leaving the house. He’s in soft pants and a t-shirt swallowed by a dark zip-up hoodie, the hood pulled down over his face as he gets into the car. It’s the first time, too, that Sokka can make out something of Zuko’s shape. It’s not that he’s ogling him. It’s more than he finds himself doing a quick scan, looking for injuries or something smaller than that, tiny clues.

He isn’t pudgy, but he’s soft all over, with curving hips and a slight belly. It’s the softness of someone who never really leaves their room. After he buckles himself in—fumbling again, but he does it—he wraps his arms around himself, like he’s trying to hide his body. It’s already betrayed him once today.

“We’re gonna go to the clinic on Fourth and Pine," Sokka says, navigating out of the parking lot. “Unless you have a preference.”

Zuko shakes his head. “I’ve been there before,” he says.

They turn right onto Pine in rush-hour traffic, and Sokka thinks, not for the first time, how lovely this city would be without anyone in it. You can see the mountains butting over the traffic lights, threads of snow snaking down the peaks. There are pine trees everywhere. It feels like home.

“Why nine?” Sokka asks.

Zuko jumps a little.

“Sorry.”

“No, I was—thinking.” Zuko pulls his sleeves down over his hands. “It’s better when we get— when I get him out the door,” he says. “After I’ve helped with… with the things. The morning. Stuff.”

He’s hunting for easy words, and Sokka is terrified.

“What’s the morning stuff?” he asks casually.

“Oh, just the… Making breakfast. Um. Cleaning up after… the breakfast.”

This close, side by side, Sokka can smell cigarettes on him.

"Do you smoke?”

“When I’m nervous,” Zuko says frankly. He giggles, covering up his mouth.

“I do too,” Sokka says, turning onto Fourth.

“I have to smoke outside, though.” Zuko sighs. “It’s lonely.”

“Cigarettes are lonely,” Sokka agrees. “I always think I’m over it, but sometimes it’s the only thing that works.”

“I think we’re never over it,” Zuko says. “Some things.”

“Right,” Sokka says, wondering what he’s really talking about.

When they park, Zuko looks surprised to see Sokka getting out of the car.

“You’re coming in?”

“Yeah,” he says. “I’ll be in the waiting room if you need anything.” He pauses. “I mean, if you want me.”

Zuko’s expression is blank.

“I think I do,” he says.

—

The clinic always manages to look worse every time Sokka sees it. The waiting room is overcrowded, the receptionist is harried but never seems to actually be doing anything, and while there isn’t a clock hanging on the wall, the stutter of a fluorescent bulb counts out the seconds. He thinks it’s probably how they keep time in hell.

Katara’s a saint, working here. He knows that. She treats the homeless, teenage addicts, queer kids who’ve run away from their families and ended up in trouble. But he also wonders sometimes if she works here just to prove that.

Eventually, with huge relief, he sees Katara escorting Zuko back to the waiting room.

“Hey sis!”

Katara looks at him. Zuko looks at him, then at Katara, then back to him. The color is draining from Zuko’s face, which is almost impressive, considering how pale he was to begin with.

“You’re," Zuko says. “Um.”

Katara’s face says he’s fucked something up, royally.

“Is that okay?” Sokka asks.

“I guess?” Zuko says.

“Please excuse me for a moment, Li,” Katara says. She pulls Sokka down the hallway by his ear and into an empty examination room.

“What are you doing?” she hisses.

“I was saying hi?”

“He’s going to think we’re colluding!”

“He’s… not wrong?”

Katara glares at him.

“I don’t think you understand the degree to which you’ve just fucked things up," she says. “This is a major breach of trust. Who knows if he’s going to come back after this? To me or to you?”

“Why wouldn’t he come b—”

“Because now he’s going to think I sent you to check on him, and he’s going to think that you were just pretending to be friendly with him so you could do that.”

“I don’t know that he’ll jump to—”

“He is an *abused* *person*, Sokka,” she whispers fiercely. “I don’t know why I have to spell this out for you. These are people who get paranoid because it *works*. It saves their *lives*. There’s absolutely no way he isn’t going to think this is weird.”

“It kind of is,” he points out.

“Oh my God. I can’t believe y—”

A nurse knocks on the door.

“Dr. Kiliak?”

“All fine!” Katara says warmly. “I’ll have the room cleared in a sec.” To Sokka she says, in her low angry voice: “We’ll just play dumb. I’ll say I had no idea you lived in the same building.”

“You didn’t,” Sokka says. “Until last—”

Katara opens the door.

“Out,” she says.

—

Zuko is sitting on a chair in the waiting room, knees drawn to his chest. With his hood down, Sokka can see he took the time to wash and comb out his hair this morning. It’s longer than he thought, still slightly damp, spilling halfway down his back. It’s beautiful hair, really.

“Hey," Sokka says. “Sorry about that.” *That* covers a lot of ground, and he hopes Zuko won’t try to figure out what, exactly, falls under it. “Did they check you out?”

Zuko shakes his head, wincing a little, and touches his temple.

“I have to wait,” he says hoarsely. “CT scan.”

“Oh.” He sits down next to Zuko, swinging his legs nervously in the chair. “How’s your head?”

“They don’t know. They have to do the scan.”

“I mean, how does it feel? Are you feeling any better?”

Zuko isn’t even looking at Sokka anymore. His eyes are closed.

“I’m tired," he says.

**You oughta tell me what you tell yourself**

“Do you want a cigarette?” Sokka asks, when they’re back in the car.

He pulls a carton out of the console and offers it to Zuko, who looks at him skeptically.

“Your sister is a doctor,” he says.

“Frankly,” Sokka says, cigarette between his teeth, “I don’t care what my doctor sister thinks.”

Zuko shakes a cigarette out of the carton and just holds it, turning it around in his fingers. They’re still quivering, although not as badly as this morning.

“I thought you two were close,” he says.

“I don’t know,” Sokka says, flicking his lighter. He lights his cigarette, then Zuko’s. “Sometimes we are. I’m kind of mad at her today.”

“Why?”

“She yelled at me.”

“Oh,” Zuko says. He’s turned stiff again, the stiffness that’s there-and-gone. He blows out a puffy trail of smoke. “She shouldn’t do that,” he says solemnly, like Sokka’s relationship with his sister is the real problem today.

Sokka shrugs.

“It seems like,” Zuko says. “Um.”

“Go on.”

“It seems like she has a temper.”

“I wouldn’t argue with that,” Sokka says. “She doesn’t go past that, though.”

Zuko tilts his head.

“She gets mean and then nothing happens,” Sokka says. “It’s fine. I just deal with it.”

“I’m sorry you have to.”

He looks at Zuko, and Zuko doesn’t look away. His eyes are sad and bright, brighter with the fire hanging off his lips.

“Can I ask you something?” Sokka says.

“Okay.”

“The answer can be no. I don’t mind.”

“It’s hard to know when I don’t know the question,” Zuko says drily.

"How about I ask you, and then you can say no if you don’t like the question.”

He didn’t mean to make a big production of it, but he has, and Zuko looks exhausted.
